\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% 5.1 Key Findings
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{What the Measurements Show}
\label{subsec:findings}

The central result is an ordering of dispersions: measured on the transfer asymmetry itself, retraining both architectures from scratch moves it by a standard deviation of $0.23$ to $1.27$ points, whereas changing only the conditioning protocol moves it by $15.01\pm0.84$ points (Section~\ref{subsec:statistics_results}). For scale on a different quantity, re-running an attack with a new random initialization moves absolute robust accuracy by about $0.05$ points and retraining moves the adversarially trained pair by at most $0.55$. A quantity that the measurement choice moves by fifteen points while retraining moves it by one is not a stable property of the architectures, and reporting it without the protocol communicates almost nothing.

One objection deserves an answer in its own terms: the four protocols estimate four different quantities, so the distance between them is not a variance, and calling it one would be a category error. We agree, and we make no assumption that the architectures have a single true asymmetry that the protocols estimate with error. Our claim concerns what a reader can recover. When a study reports one number without naming the protocol, the reader cannot tell which of the four quantities was estimated, and the spread we measure is the size of that ambiguity, a bound on what the omission costs. Conditioning itself remains established practice~\cite{liu2017delving, dong2018boosting, ravikumar2023trend} and is necessary; the point is that the choice of conditioning set has to be reported with the result, in the main text.

Whether our direction is the correct one is a separate question. Under a both-correct protocol comparable to ours, Mahmood et al.~\cite{mahmood2021robustness} report the opposite direction on a different architecture pair, finding transfer into the transformer weaker than transfer out of it, and Benz et al.~\cite{benz2021adversarial} and Shao et al.~\cite{shao2022adversarial} report the same ordering on their own model sets. Our mechanism is compatible with those results: we attribute the asymmetry to the target's own vulnerability rather than to any special strength of CNN-crafted perturbations, which is also the first of the two factors Demontis et al.~\cite{demontis2019adversarial} identify, and the weaker target in Mahmood et al.'s pair is not the weaker target in ours. Testing this properly would require their per-direction clean accuracies under a shared protocol, which is the reporting we ask for and would give the mechanism a direct external test.

The four protocols are not interchangeable, and each has a defensible use. The unconditioned rate should not be used for architecture comparison at all: our $3\times3$ matrix shows its deviation from the conditioned rate is algebraically equal to the target's clean error scaled by $(1 - r_{\text{cond}})$, which means it measures target weakness in a known proportion, not transferability. The target-correct protocol removes that confound but admits samples the source itself cannot solve, and because those marginal subsets are highly vulnerable and asymmetrically sized, it pulls two directions toward each other by an amount that depends on the clean-accuracy gap. The both-correct protocol scores both directions on identical samples and is the only one of the four that supports paired inference, which is why we treat it as the primary estimate. The successful-source protocol answers a different question, how well an attack that already worked carries over, and its larger value reflects that conditioning on source success selects strong perturbations.

Across four protocols and three training runs, CNN-crafted examples transfer better to the ViT than the reverse \emph{on both CIFAR datasets}, and on CIFAR-10 also under MI-FGSM. On SVHN, where the clean-accuracy gap is $1.85$ points (against $12.3$ and $20.7$ on the CIFAR datasets), two protocols report a CNN advantage and two a ViT advantage, and the primary both-correct protocol gives $-0.35$ points with equivalence accepted (Section~\ref{subsec:svhn}). The direction is therefore a property of the unmatched regime: there the protocol changes how large the effect looks and whether equivalence is accepted, and once the models are matched it changes the sign as well. The three-target analysis accounts for the direction without requiring anything special of CNN perturbations, since incoming transfer tracks each target's own white-box vulnerability ($r = 0.986$), and the failure of a transformer-specific attack~\cite{zhang2023tgr} to rescue the ViT direction fits the same account. With three targets that correlation is suggestive; establishing it would take a larger model pool.

The conditional decomposition separates absolute robustness into a clean-accuracy factor and a conditional-susceptibility factor. In our corrected runs both favor the CNN, so the decomposition and the headline agree. They did not agree on our earlier checkpoints, where conditional susceptibility appeared matched under PGD-10 and slightly ViT-favorable under AutoAttack. Since the absolute ordering was identical in both checkpoint sets, a paper reporting only absolute accuracy would have seen nothing change, while a paper reporting the decomposition from a single run would have drawn a mechanistic conclusion that does not replicate.

Sparsity is not spatial locality: adversarially trained CNN gradients concentrate more mass on fewer components (Hoyer $0.4928$ versus $0.4561$, consistent in every run; per-sample $p<10^{-11}$, run-level $p=0.108$ after correction), but four spatial descriptors of the same gradients show no corresponding difference in how those components are arranged in the image. Descriptions of CNN gradients as spatially localized therefore need separate evidence; sparsity measures~\cite{hurley2009comparing} do not provide it.

Adversarial perturbation does not measurably change CLS attention entropy. At $n=1000$ the per-layer entropy change is at most $0.005$ nats, while attention maps are displaced by a small and depth-increasing amount. Attention-shift results obtained with patch attacks~\cite{gu2022vision, fu2022patchfool} concern a different threat model and should not be generalized to $L_\infty$-bounded perturbations without measurement.

A ViT-specific transfer attack does not improve ViT-sourced transfer here. TGR~\cite{zhang2023tgr} costs 14.9 points of white-box strength and returns none of it at the target in our regime. The result is specific to this regime, since our targets are adversarially trained and our data are CIFAR-scale.

Two behavioral differences reproduce across independent checkpoint sets and are unaffected by the protocol question. CNN input gradients are sparser under all three scale-invariant measures, an ordering that adversarial training creates (the clean-trained pair has it reversed), and cross-sample alignment is higher for the ViT in absolute cosine while the signed mean is near zero for both. Samples thus share sensitivity axes but not directions, which separates our measurement from a universal-perturbation claim~\cite{moosavi2017universal}. The depth profiles differ as well: in the CNN the damage is concentrated in the last residual stage, whereas in the ViT it builds up gradually as a rotation of the representation. That the ViT's measured profile depends on token aggregation, since the CLS pathway drifts about three times as far as the patch-token mean, is itself a reporting requirement for layer-wise studies.

\subsection{Reporting Requirements}
\label{subsec:implications}

Several requirements for cross-architecture robustness studies follow from these measurements.

Studies should state the conditioning protocol and report which samples enter the denominator. Where a directional claim is made, a protocol that scores both directions on identical samples should be preferred, so that paired inference is available. Unconditioned transfer rates should not be reported as architecture comparisons. When targets differ in clean accuracy, the raw rate is a known mixture of transferability and target weakness, $r_{\text{raw}} - r_{\text{cond}} = e\,(1 - r_{\text{cond}})$, and since that relation is an algebraic identity, the correction can be applied to published raw rates without re-running anything.

Next to the score, studies should report the decomposition. Absolute robustness factors exactly into clean accuracy and conditional susceptibility, and which factor carries a headline is not recoverable from the score alone. Ranking claims should be separated from mechanism claims: our rankings were stable across checkpoint sets while our mechanism attribution was not, so mechanism claims should carry more training runs. For layer-wise analyses the aggregation should be stated, since CLS-only, patch-mean, and all-token summaries of the same blocks give different drift magnitudes and different minima. Attention claims, finally, should be checked at a realistic sample size: the attention null result we report had looked positive on a small figure batch.

For practitioners choosing between the two families under matched standard adversarial training on this benchmark, the CNN gives higher absolute robust accuracy and the advantage holds under every view we measured. The WideResNet reference at 62.76\% shows that a larger model trained on extra data with a stronger recipe~\cite{gowal2020uncovering} moves robustness further than the choice between these two families does.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

The scope of the study limits its conclusions in specific ways.

The protocol-sensitivity result is measured on three datasets and one model pair. On CIFAR-100 the spread stays close to the CIFAR-10 value and the sign is preserved throughout (Section~\ref{subsec:cifar100}). The effect is therefore not CIFAR-specific, but its size does not follow the clean-accuracy gap across datasets: CIFAR-100 has by far the larger gap and the slightly smaller spread. The mechanism we propose predicts that a gap produces a spread, not how the spread orders between datasets. That account is incomplete: it holds for three of the four protocols, while the successful-source protocol adds a second driver tied to the \emph{source's} robustness that is strong enough to reverse the overall relationship (Section~\ref{subsec:transfer}). What we still cannot state is the functional form of either dependence with confidence: the estimates rest on six seed-and-dataset clusters, and every small-gap pair in our pool involves the external WideResNet, so a small clean-accuracy gap and a different training recipe are not separable here. A study spanning several architecture pairs with varying clean-accuracy gaps would turn these measurements into a calibration curve.

We cannot isolate the cause of the differences between our two checkpoint sets. The corrected protocol changed the validation handling and produced a fresh training run at the same time. A data-quantity explanation is ruled out, since the corrected clean models are better despite less data, but distinguishing selection leakage from ordinary run-to-run variation would require an ablation that trains on identical data with and without a leaked selection split. The contrast is evidence that single-run mechanism claims are fragile; it does not measure leakage.

Every model in this paper is trained inside a single threat model: $\Linf$ at $\eps = 8/255$. We evaluated the same checkpoints under an $L_2$ budget and the protocol spread survived, at $10.92$ against $15.01$ points (Section~\ref{subsec:l2}), but that test probes how the protocol affects the conclusion, not $L_2$ robustness, and its absolute values are not comparable with $L_2$-trained references. Whether the effect holds for models \emph{trained} under another norm is untested here.

Three runs per architecture bound seed variance but do not span the recipe space, and ViT adversarial training is known to be recipe-sensitive on small datasets~\cite{mo2022adversarial, debenedetti2023light}; and a ViT trained with the diffusion-generated data recipe of Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023better} has been reported to surpass WideResNets on CIFAR-10~\cite{wu2025vision}. Our ViT results characterize baseline adversarial training, not the best achievable ViT robustness. The ViT-Tiny pipeline upsamples $32\times32$ inputs to $224\times224$ inside the model; our CIFAR-native control shows the gradient ordering is not an upsampling artifact, but that control differs in capacity and patch size and therefore calibrates the confound without providing a second matched pair. The two models also differ in parameter count (11.2M versus 5.7M), so architectural attribution of the absolute gap remains partial. Our TGR result, finally, comes from an adaptation of the original method, not a reproduction of its published setting.

\subsection{Future Directions}
\label{subsec:future}

The most direct extension is to turn the protocol-sensitivity measurement into a general one: sweep architecture pairs with controlled clean-accuracy gaps and measure how the spread across protocols scales with that gap. If the relationship is as regular as our three-target correlation suggests, it would yield a correction that lets published raw-rate asymmetries be reinterpreted rather than discarded.

Two natural extensions are already part of this paper. The leakage ablation, on six full-budget trajectories, did not detect an effect of leakage on checkpoint selection, but it answers a weaker question than the one posed: the treatment was applied to a single validation split, so split identity and leakage are not separable within that design, and what it does yield is the selection-protocol dispersion of Section~\ref{subsec:statistics_results}. The other is the second dataset of Section~\ref{subsec:cifar100}. What remains open is the capacity-matched pair (ViT-Small against ResNet-50, say), which would test whether the sparsity ordering and the depth profiles are properties of the families or of these particular small models.
