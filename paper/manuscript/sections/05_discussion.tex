% 5.1 Key Findings

\subsection{Key Findings and Their Implications}
\label{subsec:findings}

\paragraph{Transfer Asymmetry Is a Property of the Measurement Protocol as Much as of the Models}

Our experiments sharpen a methodological point that we believe deserves more attention in cross-architecture transfer studies. Conditioning transfer evaluation on correctly classified samples is established practice~\cite{liu2017delving, dong2018boosting, ravikumar2023trend}; what our controlled same-experiment comparison adds is the demonstration that \emph{which} conditioning protocol one adopts can qualitatively change the conclusion on the same data (Table~\ref{tab:transfer_protocols}). The raw metric manufactures an 8.3-point asymmetry favoring CNN sources out of the targets' ten-point clean-accuracy difference. The target-correct conditioned metric removes that confound and shows near-parity (20.95\% vs 20.32\%, TOST-equivalent within $\pm$2 points). Yet the both-correct and successful-source protocols---the former used by careful prior work~\cite{mahmood2021robustness, ravikumar2023trend}---reveal a significant 5.3-point CNN$\rightarrow$ViT advantage on the jointly solved samples. No single number here is ``the'' transfer asymmetry: the raw gap is an artifact, the target-correct parity reflects a compositional cancellation (marginal samples that only the target solves are hyper-vulnerable in both directions and asymmetrically numerous), and the both-correct gap is the cleanest paired estimate of directional transferability on shared inputs. We therefore recommend that cross-architecture transfer studies state their conditioning protocol explicitly, justify it, and where feasible report several---treating protocol sensitivity itself as a result rather than a nuisance.

This reframing also changes the interpretive link to our gradient measurements. Under the both-correct protocol, adversarial examples crafted on the sparser-gradient CNN transfer better to the ViT than vice versa; the direction co-occurs with the gradient-structure differences we measure, but our correlational design cannot attribute it, and the target-correct parity shows the aggregate picture is protocol-dependent. Mahmood et al.~\cite{mahmood2021robustness} observe cross-architecture asymmetries under a both-correct protocol for normally trained models; our both-correct result is directionally consistent with theirs, while our raw-metric analysis shows how easily a differently manufactured asymmetry could be mistaken for such a finding when targets differ in clean accuracy.

\paragraph{Gradient Characteristics as Vulnerability Signatures}

The gradient analysis characterizes how the two architectures distribute input sensitivity. Across three scale-invariant measures~\cite{hurley2009comparing}, CNN gradients are consistently sparser than ViT gradients (Hoyer 0.474 vs 0.449; Gini 0.634 vs 0.606; 31.5\% vs 25.8\% of components below 1\% of the per-sample maximum; paired Wilcoxon Holm-corrected $p < 10^{-15}$, paired Cohen's $d$ 0.34--0.65), indicating modestly more concentrated sensitivity---consistent with the known tendency of adversarial training to sparsify input gradients~\cite{chalasani2020concise} and to reshape gradient structure more broadly~\cite{tsipras2019robustness}, here differing in degree between architectures. We deliberately phrase this as concentration in fewer input \emph{components}: whether the concentration is also spatially localized is a distinct claim that our sparsity measures do not test. Two controls strengthen this reading. First, the native-resolution ViT control is even less sparse than the upsampled ViT, so the ordering is not an artifact of input upsampling. Second, the effect size is honest: an earlier threshold-based analysis (on the preliminary run2 models) suggested a 1.7$\times$ sparsity ratio, most of which we traced to gradient-scale differences rather than structure---the structural difference is real but small. In parallel, cross-sample gradient alignment is measurably higher for the ViT (0.052 vs 0.038, all-pairs absolute cosine), suggesting somewhat more shared sensitivity directions across inputs, though both values are low in absolute terms.

The alignment result merits careful interpretation. Universal adversarial perturbations~\cite{moosavi2017universal} exploit sensitivity directions shared across inputs; one might therefore hypothesize that ViTs' global self-attention produces higher cross-sample gradient alignment. We observe a difference in that direction---ViT alignment is 1.36$\times$ the CNN's (0.052 vs 0.038), and the native-resolution ViT control is higher still (0.079)---but two caveats bound the interpretation. First, the absolute levels are low for both architectures, far from the strong alignment that would make universal perturbations cheap. Second, the \emph{signed} mean cosine is near zero for every model ($\leq$0.002): samples share sensitivity axes but not consistent directions, whereas universal perturbations require sign-coherent alignment---so the measured gap concerns the geometry of sensitivity subspaces rather than directly predicting universal-perturbation vulnerability. Measuring the same quantity in the clean-trained counterparts resolves the training-regime question directly: clean alignment is 0.050 for the ViT and 0.030 for the CNN, essentially the same levels as after adversarial training. The ViT--CNN alignment gap is therefore an \emph{architectural} property that adversarial training neither creates nor suppresses appreciably in our setting---in contrast to gradient magnitude, which adversarial training reduces by an order of magnitude for both models, and to sparsity, whose architecture ordering adversarial training reverses (Section~\ref{subsec:gradient}).

\paragraph{Feature Degradation in Vision Transformers}

Our layer-wise analysis reveals a characteristic feature degradation profile in ViT layers. In the first two transformer blocks, adversarial inputs produce noticeable $L_2$ distance from clean representations while maintaining semantic direction (cosine similarity above 0.99) and near-constant norms; the perturbation initially displaces representations without appreciably altering either their norm or their semantic direction. Directional deviation then accumulates rapidly through the early-to-middle network, with cosine similarity reaching its minimum of 0.955 at Block~8, after which it plateaus with a mild recovery in the final blocks (0.965 at Blocks~10--11); one untested hypothesis for the recovery is a regularizing influence of the classifier head. The degradation is subtler than the attack's effectiveness might suggest---norm changes remain below 1.5\% throughout---implying that modest directional shifts, largely established by mid-network, are sufficient to flip predictions on most attacked samples.

This early-drop, mid-network-plateau profile suggests that effective defenses should focus on stabilizing early-to-middle layer representations rather than only filtering perturbations at the input level or protecting the final layers. Intermediate supervision or layer-wise consistency regularization might help maintain representational integrity throughout the network. The parallel residual-block analysis of the adversarially trained CNN (Section~\ref{subsec:attention}) confirms the profile is specific to the evaluated model pair rather than generic: the CNN's representations degrade monotonically with depth and with large terminal norm distortions, suggesting the two models would benefit from differently placed stabilization.

\subsection{Broader Implications}
\label{subsec:implications}

\paragraph{For Researchers}

Our work demonstrates the value of multi-view behavioral analysis over single-number benchmarking. Simply reporting that one architecture achieves higher robust accuracy than another provides limited insight: as our conditional decomposition shows, the same models support the readings ``the CNN is more robust'' (absolute and both-correct views), ``conditional susceptibility is matched'' (per-model PGD view), and ``the ViT loses less on its solved samples'' (per-model AutoAttack view), depending on the conditioning. Characterizing \textit{how} architectures behave differently---through protocol-explicit transfer analysis, gradient characterization, and feature degradation tracking---enables targeted improvements rather than trial-and-error defense development.

The consistent gap between AutoAttack and PGD-10 results (3.1--5.2 percentage points lower robust accuracy under AutoAttack for our models; Section~\ref{subsec:robustness}) reinforces the importance of using standardized, strong evaluation protocols. While PGD-10 remains useful during development for its computational efficiency, final robustness claims should always be validated with AutoAttack or equivalent comprehensive evaluation. Beyond this, conditioned transfer rates measured from diverse source architectures could serve as a diagnostic for architecture-specific vulnerabilities---a direction our two-model study cannot itself establish, but one that the clean-accuracy-controlled methodology makes feasible.

\paragraph{For Practitioners}

For deployment scenarios requiring adversarial robustness, our findings offer concrete guidance. Under matched standard adversarial training on this benchmark, the CNN provides better absolute robust accuracy with more stable training dynamics; note, however, that most of its absolute PGD advantage reflects its higher clean accuracy rather than lower conditional susceptibility, so the practical choice also depends on which accuracy profile a deployment values. ViTs require more careful hyperparameter tuning during adversarial training and may benefit from specialized techniques beyond standard adversarial training.

When deploying ensemble defenses that combine CNN and ViT models, practitioners should evaluate transfer under an explicitly chosen, clean-accuracy-controlled protocol---and ideally more than one---before assuming either architecture is the harder surrogate; our analysis shows that the raw metric manufactures an asymmetry, the target-correct metric shows parity, and the both-correct metric reveals a genuine CNN$\rightarrow$ViT advantage on jointly solved samples. Additionally, the gap between WideResNet-28-10 (62.76\% robust accuracy) and ResNet-18 (35.74\%) shows how much headroom lies beyond our baselines---though this particular model owes its robustness to capacity, additional (unlabeled) training data, and a stronger training recipe jointly~\cite{gowal2020uncovering}, so the gap should not be read as a capacity effect alone. For high-stakes applications, investing in larger models trained with strong recipes and augmented data may prove more effective than developing novel defense mechanisms.

\paragraph{On Model Capacity}

Our comparison involves models with different parameter counts: ResNet-18 has 11.2M parameters while ViT-Tiny has 5.7M. This $2\times$ capacity difference could partially explain the robustness gap. However, our mechanistic analyses suggest that architectural factors play a more fundamental role than model size alone.

While parameter count differs (11.2M vs.\ 5.7M), our gradient analysis reveals scale-invariant structural differences---modestly sparser CNN gradients and moderately higher ViT absolute alignment---that are consistent with architectural processing differences and survive the native-resolution control. The transfer evidence is protocol-dependent (parity under target-correct conditioning, a CNN$\rightarrow$ViT advantage under both-correct conditioning), so whether gradient-level differences translate into directional transfer effects cannot be settled by our design; architectural attribution of the robust-accuracy gap itself likewise remains partial: capacity, clean-accuracy differences, and training-recipe maturity for ViTs on small datasets all plausibly contribute. We acknowledge that capacity-controlled experiments (e.g., using ViT-Small with $\sim$22M parameters) would provide stronger evidence for disentangling these factors. We leave such experiments for future work.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

Several limitations constrain the scope of our conclusions and suggest directions for future validation.

Our ViT-Tiny model uses $224 \times 224$ bilinear upsampling from CIFAR-10's native $32 \times 32$ resolution. Although this input pipeline mirrors how ImageNet-scale ViT architectures are commonly adapted to small images, our model is trained from scratch (no ImageNet pretraining), so the upsampling is an architectural-adaptation choice rather than a requirement of pretrained weights. Upsampling could in principle confound input-gradient statistics, since each input pixel influences many backbone positions. Our CIFAR-native ViT control (Section~\ref{subsec:gradient}) addresses this directly: the native-resolution model is even less sparse and more aligned than the upsampled one, so the CNN--ViT ordering of gradient characteristics is not an upsampling artifact. The control model differs in capacity and patch size, however, so it calibrates the preprocessing confound rather than providing a second matched comparison; the semantic drift analysis likewise reflects the upsampled pipeline.

Our evaluation focuses exclusively on CIFAR-10, and findings may differ on ImageNet where ViTs typically excel and pretrained robust models are available. We evaluate only $L_\infty$ attacks; $L_2$ or semantic attacks may reveal different vulnerability patterns. We validate evaluation stability across three random seeds for attack initialization (standard deviation below 0.25 percentage points) and report multiple training runs per architecture (Section~\ref{subsec:statistics_results}), though additional training runs under the final protocol would be needed to attach uncertainty estimates to the architecture comparison itself. Finally, standard adversarial training for ViT produces lower robust accuracy than specialized techniques such as diffusion-based data augmentation, with which ViTs have recently been reported to surpass WideResNets on small-scale datasets~\cite{wang2023better, wu2025vision}; our ViT results represent baseline adversarial training performance rather than state-of-the-art ViT robustness, and our architecture comparison holds specifically under matched standard adversarial training.

\subsection{Future Directions}
\label{subsec:future}

Our findings motivate several research directions. Systematically evaluating CNN-ViT hybrid architectures such as ConViT and CoAtNet could reveal whether combining architectural elements yields robustness benefits beyond either paradigm alone. The gradient sparsity difference we identify---CNN gradients modestly more concentrated under scale-invariant measures, ViT gradients somewhat more spatially distributed---suggests that hybrids might achieve more balanced vulnerability profiles.

Developing training losses that explicitly encourage attention pattern stability under perturbation may address the vulnerability correlates we characterize in ViTs. The early-accumulating semantic drift we observe (largely established by mid-network) suggests that intermediate-layer supervision or consistency regularization could help maintain feature integrity throughout the network.

Investigating whether the robustness gap persists at larger scales (ViT-Base, ViT-Large) and on higher-resolution datasets would establish the generality of our conclusions. Finally, extending our analysis to certified defense methods, including randomized smoothing and interval bound propagation, would determine whether the architectural differences we identify affect achievable certification radii.
