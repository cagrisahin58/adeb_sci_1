\section{Discussion}

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% 5.1 Key Findings
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Key Findings and Their Implications}
\label{subsec:findings}

\paragraph{Transfer Attack Asymmetry}

Our experiments reveal a consistent asymmetry in cross-architecture adversarial transfer. Adversarial examples generated on CNN transfer to ViT with 41.2\% success rate (68.8\% transfer efficiency), while ViT-generated examples transfer to CNN at 36.1\% (57.6\% efficiency). The 11.2 percentage point gap in transfer efficiency, while more moderate than some prior cross-architecture transfer studies might suggest, is directionally stable across evaluation seeds and training runs.

The asymmetry has a partial mechanistic explanation rooted in gradient structure. CNN gradients are 1.7$\times$ more sparse than ViT gradients, concentrating sensitivity in localized regions that correspond to generic image features such as edges and textures. Because both architectures rely on these low-level features, perturbations crafted to exploit CNN vulnerabilities tend to affect ViTs as well. ViT gradients, being more uniformly distributed, produce perturbations that spread across the input and are less effective at targeting the spatially concentrated features that CNN processing depends on. Interestingly, cross-sample gradient alignment is nearly identical between architectures (0.053 vs 0.055), indicating that the transfer asymmetry cannot be attributed to alignment differences alone. The sparsity of CNN gradients---not the alignment of ViT gradients---appears to be the primary driver. This finding refines the common assumption that ViT gradient alignment underlies transfer vulnerability, and has practical implications for adversarial defense: in black-box scenarios, CNNs remain the more effective surrogate model, but the advantage is more modest than gradient alignment alone would predict.

\paragraph{Gradient Characteristics as Vulnerability Signatures}

The gradient analysis provides insight into why CNN-generated perturbations are more transferable. CNNs exhibit 7.24\% near-zero gradient components compared to 4.15\% for ViTs---a 1.7$\times$ difference indicating that CNN sensitivity is more concentrated in specific spatial regions while ViT sensitivity is comparatively more spread across the input. This sparsity gap, though narrower than initial hypotheses suggested, is consistent with the moderate transfer asymmetry we observe.

A finding that merits careful interpretation is the near-identical cross-sample gradient alignment between architectures (0.053 vs 0.055). This contradicts the expectation, suggested by prior work on universal adversarial perturbations~\cite{moosavi2017universal}, that ViTs' global self-attention mechanism would produce substantially higher gradient alignment. Under adversarial training, both architectures appear to converge toward similar alignment levels, possibly because the adversarial training objective itself regularizes gradient directions. This result suggests that vulnerability to universal perturbations may depend less on architecture and more on training regime---a hypothesis that future work with varied training configurations could test.

\paragraph{Feature Degradation in Vision Transformers}

Our layer-wise analysis reveals a progressive feature degradation pattern in ViT layers. In early transformer blocks (0--2), adversarial inputs produce high $L_2$ distance from clean representations but maintain semantic direction with cosine similarity exceeding 0.99. The perturbation affects magnitude without corrupting semantic content. As representations propagate through the network, this pattern shifts: late layers (9--11) show cosine similarity dropping to 0.958 at Block~10, indicating progressive semantic corruption. The degradation is subtler than the attack's effectiveness might suggest---norm changes remain below 1.5\% throughout---implying that even small per-layer directional shifts compound across twelve transformer blocks into sufficient deviation for misclassification. The partial recovery at Block~11 (cosine similarity rising to 0.964) likely reflects the regularizing influence of the classifier head on the final transformer block.

This degradation pattern suggests that effective defenses should focus on stabilizing late-layer representations rather than filtering perturbations at the input level. Intermediate supervision or layer-wise consistency regularization might help maintain semantic integrity throughout the network. We note that the absence of a comparable layer-wise analysis for CNN limits our ability to determine whether this phenomenon is ViT-specific; a parallel study of residual block representations in CNNs would provide valuable contrast and is an important direction for future work.

\subsection{Broader Implications}
\label{subsec:implications}

\paragraph{For Researchers}

Our work demonstrates the value of mechanistic analysis over pure benchmarking. Simply reporting that one architecture achieves higher robust accuracy than another provides limited insight for improving defenses. Understanding \textit{why} architectures behave differently---through gradient analysis, transfer studies, and feature degradation tracking---enables targeted improvements rather than trial-and-error defense development.

The consistent gap between AutoAttack and PGD-10 results (6--7\% lower robust accuracy under AutoAttack) reinforces the importance of using standardized, strong evaluation protocols. While PGD-10 remains useful during development for its computational efficiency, final robustness claims should always be validated with AutoAttack or equivalent comprehensive evaluation. Transfer attack analysis also emerges from our work as a valuable diagnostic tool: when a model shows low transfer rates from diverse source architectures, this indicates architecture-specific vulnerabilities that merit dedicated investigation.

\paragraph{For Practitioners}

For deployment scenarios requiring adversarial robustness, our findings offer concrete guidance. CNNs currently provide better robust accuracy with more stable adversarial training dynamics, making them the safer choice when robustness is paramount. ViTs require more careful hyperparameter tuning during adversarial training and may benefit from specialized techniques beyond standard adversarial training.

When deploying ensemble defenses that combine CNN and ViT models, practitioners should be aware that such combinations protect more effectively against ViT-generated attacks than CNN-generated ones due to the transfer asymmetry we observe. Additionally, our comparison between WideResNet-28-10 (62.76\% robust accuracy) and ResNet-18 (36.0\%) demonstrates that model capacity substantially impacts robustness. Investing in larger models may prove more effective than developing novel defense mechanisms, particularly for high-stakes applications.

\paragraph{On Model Capacity}

Our comparison involves models with different parameter counts: ResNet-18 has 11.2M parameters while ViT-Tiny has 5.7M. This $2\times$ capacity difference could partially explain the robustness gap. However, our mechanistic analyses suggest that architectural factors play a more fundamental role than model size alone.

While parameter count differs (11.2M vs.\ 5.7M), our gradient analysis reveals qualitative differences in gradient structure---particularly sparsity patterns---that are consistent with architectural processing differences rather than pure capacity effects. The 1.7$\times$ sparsity difference reflects how each architecture computes input gradients: CNNs concentrate sensitivity through local receptive fields, while ViTs distribute it through global self-attention. That said, the near-identical gradient alignment (0.053 vs 0.055) complicates a purely architectural explanation---under adversarial training, both architectures converge toward similar alignment levels despite their different attention mechanisms. The transfer asymmetry, though moderate, remains directionally consistent with the sparsity difference and supports the interpretation that architectural computation shapes vulnerability patterns beyond what parameter count alone would predict. We acknowledge that capacity-controlled experiments (e.g., using ViT-Small with $\sim$22M parameters) would provide stronger evidence for disentangling architectural from capacity effects. We leave such experiments for future work.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:limitations}

Several limitations constrain the scope of our conclusions and suggest directions for future validation.

Our ViT-Tiny model uses $224 \times 224$ upsampling from CIFAR-10's native $32 \times 32$ resolution, following standard practice for leveraging pretrained ImageNet backbones. This design choice introduces potential confounds: the upsampling may introduce artifacts that affect adversarial vulnerability differently than native-resolution processing. We note, however, that this setup reflects common deployment practice where pretrained ViTs are adapted to smaller images. Moreover, our gradient and transfer analyses reveal architectural differences in sparsity patterns that are unlikely to be artifacts of resolution, and the semantic drift phenomenon operates at the feature level independent of input preprocessing. Nevertheless, future work should validate these findings using CIFAR-native ViT architectures with smaller patch sizes to isolate architectural effects from resolution effects.

Our evaluation focuses exclusively on CIFAR-10, and findings may differ on ImageNet where ViTs typically excel and pretrained robust models are available. We evaluate only $L_\infty$ attacks; $L_2$ or semantic attacks may reveal different vulnerability patterns. We validate evaluation stability across three random seeds (showing less than 0.25\% variance) and report two independent training runs per architecture (Section~\ref{subsec:statistics_results}), though additional runs would further strengthen training-level reproducibility claims. Finally, standard adversarial training for ViT produces lower robust accuracy than specialized techniques such as diffusion-based augmentation; our ViT results represent baseline adversarial training performance rather than state-of-the-art ViT robustness.

\subsection{Future Directions}
\label{subsec:future}

Our findings motivate several research directions. Systematically evaluating CNN-ViT hybrid architectures such as ConViT and CoAtNet could reveal whether combining architectural elements yields robustness benefits beyond either paradigm alone. The gradient sparsity difference we identify---localized gradients in CNNs versus distributed gradients in ViTs---suggests that hybrids might achieve more balanced vulnerability profiles.

Developing training losses that explicitly encourage attention pattern stability under perturbation may address the fundamental vulnerability mechanism we identify in ViTs. The progressive semantic drift we observe suggests that intermediate-layer supervision or consistency regularization could help maintain feature integrity throughout the network.

Investigating whether the robustness gap persists at larger scales (ViT-Base, ViT-Large) and on higher-resolution datasets would establish the generality of our conclusions. Finally, extending our analysis to certified defense methods, including randomized smoothing and interval bound propagation, would determine whether the architectural differences we identify affect achievable certification radii.
