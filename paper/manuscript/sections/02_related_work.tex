\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% 2.1 Adversarial Attacks
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Adversarial Attacks}
\label{subsec:attacks}

Adversarial attacks aim to find minimal perturbations that cause misclassification. The Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM)~\cite{goodfellow2015explaining} generates perturbations in a single step by following the sign of the loss gradient, with the perturbation magnitude bounded under the $\Linf$ norm. Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)~\cite{madry2018towards} extends FGSM through iterative optimization, taking multiple gradient-sign steps and projecting back onto the $\eps$-ball after each step (formal definitions in Section~\ref{subsec:attack_methods}).

The Carlini \& Wagner (C\&W) attack~\cite{carlini2017towards} formulates adversarial example generation as an optimization problem, often achieving higher success rates but at increased computational cost. More recently, AutoAttack~\cite{croce2020reliable} combines multiple attack strategies (APGD-CE, APGD-T, FAB-T, Square Attack) to provide a reliable robustness evaluation, becoming the de facto standard for benchmarking.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% 2.2 Defense Mechanisms
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Defense Mechanisms}
\label{subsec:defenses}

Adversarial training (AT)~\cite{madry2018towards} remains the most effective defense, training models on adversarial examples generated during each batch:
\begin{equation}
    \min_\theta \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \max_{\|\delta\|_\infty \leq \eps} \mathcal{L}(f_\theta(x + \delta), y) \right]
\end{equation}

TRADES~\cite{zhang2019theoretically} provides a theoretically-grounded trade-off between natural accuracy and robustness:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{TRADES}} = \mathcal{L}_{CE}(f(x), y) + \beta \cdot \text{KL}(f(x) \| f(x_{adv}))
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ controls the trade-off parameter.

MART~\cite{wang2020improving} addresses the issue of misclassified examples by emphasizing them during training. Other approaches include input transformation~\cite{guo2018countering}, certified defenses via randomized smoothing~\cite{cohen2019certified}, and ensemble methods~\cite{tramer2018ensemble}. Recent advances in certified robustness include Adaptive Randomized Smoothing~\cite{lyu2024adaptive}, which extends randomized smoothing to certify test-time adaptive, multi-step models.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% 2.3 CNN Robustness Studies
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{CNN Robustness}
\label{subsec:cnn_robustness}

For CNNs, Madry et al.~\cite{madry2018towards} demonstrated that adversarial training with PGD attacks provides strong empirical robustness. The RobustBench leaderboard~\cite{croce2021robustbench} tracks robust accuracy across architectures, and WideResNet variants occupy the top of its CIFAR-10 ranking.

Studies have shown that model capacity correlates with achievable robustness~\cite{madry2018towards}, with wider and deeper networks demonstrating improved robust accuracy. Architecture-specific findings include the role of skip connections in adversarial transferability~\cite{wu2020skip} and the distinct batch-normalization statistics that adversarial training induces at scale~\cite{xie2020intriguing}; relatedly, auxiliary batch-normalization branches allow adversarial examples to improve clean accuracy~\cite{xie2020adversarial}. Adversarial training is also known to reshape input-gradient structure itself, yielding more perceptually aligned gradients~\cite{tsipras2019robustness} and sparser feature attributions~\cite{chalasani2020concise}.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% 2.4 ViT Robustness Studies
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Vision Transformer Robustness}
\label{subsec:vit_robustness}

Vision Transformers~\cite{dosovitskiy2021image}, building on the self-attention mechanism introduced by Vaswani et al.~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, process images as sequences of patches; their internal representations differ measurably from CNNs' layer by layer~\cite{raghu2021vision}. Bhojanapalli et al.~\cite{bhojanapalli2021understanding} found ViTs at least as robust as ResNets across a broad range of perturbations when pre-trained on enough data. Bai et al.~\cite{bai2021transformers} systematically investigated whether transformers are more robust than CNNs, finding that under fair training recipes transformers are not more adversarially robust than CNNs, while exhibiting superior robustness under distribution shift attributed to self-attention; Benz et al.~\cite{benz2021adversarial} reported an early direct comparison including MLP-Mixer and linked the differences to frequency preferences. Subsequent work revealed that ViTs remain vulnerable to adversarial attacks, particularly gradient-based methods~\cite{mahmood2021robustness}, with patch-targeted attacks exploiting the attention mechanism directly~\cite{fu2022patchfool} and attention shown to shift measurably under patch perturbations~\cite{gu2022vision}.

Shao et al.~\cite{shao2022adversarial} found ViTs more robust than CNNs and MLP-Mixers in white-box and transfer settings, with features that carry less high-frequency content, and showed that adversarial training applies to ViTs, although in their preliminary CIFAR-10 run the adversarially trained ViT did not improve on a large CNN. ViT robustness to common corruptions and occlusions has also been characterized~\cite{paul2022vision}. More recently, Jain et al.~\cite{jain2024towards} traced a form of gradient masking in ViTs to softmax underflow in attention, which leaves PGD-style attacks sub-optimal on them, a caveat for PGD-only ViT evaluations that AutoAttack, with its gradient-free Square component, partly guards against; the adversarial-training recipe they build on the corrected attack improves on existing ViT recipes on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet-100; with diffusion-augmented adversarial training, ViTs have even been reported to surpass WideResNets on small-scale datasets~\cite{wu2025vision}. Zhu et al.~\cite{zhu2024enhancing} strengthened transferable attacks on ViTs through gradient normalization scaling and high-frequency adaptation. Behavioral comparisons between CNN and ViT robustness under a single evaluation protocol remain scarce.

% 2.5 Transfer Attacks
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Transfer Attacks}
\label{subsec:transfer_related}

Adversarial examples often transfer between models, enabling black-box attacks~\cite{papernot2016transferability}. Demontis et al.~\cite{demontis2019adversarial} trace transferability to two factors, the intrinsic vulnerability of the target model and the complexity of the surrogate, and connect it to the alignment of the two models' input gradients. Foundational transfer studies already evaluate transferability on correctly classified samples~\cite{liu2017delving}, and standard attack papers condition their success rates for the same reason~\cite{dong2018boosting}; systematic re-evaluations of transfer attacks likewise restrict the pool to samples that every model classifies correctly~\cite{zhao2025revisiting}. Two recent benchmarks move in the same direction: TA-Bench~\cite{li2023tabench} standardizes the comparison of more than thirty transfer attacks over a common pool of substitute and victim models, and Yu et al.~\cite{yu2025reliable} report that transferability is systematically overestimated when it is measured across a single architecture family, and propose three evaluation protocols that differ in how a multi-target outcome is scored: treating an ensemble as a single target, counting how many targets are fooled, and requiring that all of them are. TA-Bench holds the scoring rule fixed and varies the attack and the model pool; Yu et al.\ vary the scoring rule along the target-aggregation axis. We vary it along the conditioning axis instead, with the models, the data and the attack budget fixed, which isolates the contribution of the conditioning set and makes it possible to state the arithmetic relation between the resulting estimates. In ensemble design, Ravikumar et al.~\cite{ravikumar2023trend} restrict transfer measurement to samples correctly classified by both models precisely to account for accuracy differences between them, the same confound our conditioned metric controls.

Mahmood et al.~\cite{mahmood2021robustness} measured transfer between CNNs and ViTs with a both-correct protocol and found notably low CNN$\leftrightarrow$ViT transferability; Naseer et al.~\cite{naseer2022improving} showed that adversarial examples crafted \emph{on} ViTs transfer poorly under conventional attacks and proposed self-ensemble and token refinement to improve ViT-sourced transferability, and Wei et al.~\cite{wei2022towards} improved ViT-sourced transfer by bypassing attention gradients and patch-wise dropout. Building on these, Token Gradient Regularization (TGR)~\cite{zhang2023tgr} crafts transferable adversarial samples by exploiting ViT's attention components, while Momentum Integrated Gradients (MIG)~\cite{ma2023transferable} achieves improved transferability across both ViTs and CNNs. Chen et al.~\cite{chen2023understanding} propose a transfer attack that exploits the differing inductive biases of CNNs and ViTs; the frequency preferences behind that difference, CNNs leaning on high-frequency and ViTs on low-frequency content, are documented by Benz et al.~\cite{benz2021adversarial} and Shao et al.~\cite{shao2022adversarial}. Waseda et al.~\cite{waseda2023closer} separate transfer outcomes further, by whether the target reproduces the source's exact error or fails in a different way, and report that both occur even between closely related models.

% 2.6 Gap and Our Position
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Research Gap}
\label{subsec:gap}

While individual studies have examined CNN and ViT robustness separately, and the pitfalls of unfair CNN and transformer comparisons have been highlighted before~\cite{pinto2022impartial, bai2021transformers}, existing comparative work typically reports robustness scores in isolation, often with inconsistent perturbation budgets, attack configurations, or model scales, and rarely pairs the comparison with behavioral analysis. A recent study in this journal compares ViT and CNN adversarial robustness directly~\cite{ali2024adversarial}; our work differs by evaluating on the full test set with AutoAttack and paired statistics, by controlling transfer measurement for clean-accuracy differences, and by accompanying the comparison with gradient- and feature-level behavioral analyses.

The evaluation-methodology literature has repeatedly shown that how robustness is measured decides what is concluded: Carlini et al.~\cite{carlini2019evaluating} catalog the failure modes of robustness evaluation, and Tram\`er et al.~\cite{tramer2020adaptive} show that defenses judged robust under fixed attacks fall to adaptive ones. That work concerns attack strength, that is, whether the measurement is strong enough. The question we ask is orthogonal: with attack strength held fixed, how much does the \emph{conditioning set} alone move the conclusion? Conditioning transfer evaluation on samples the models classify correctly is established practice~\cite{liu2017delving, dong2018boosting, mahmood2021robustness, ravikumar2023trend}, and individual papers pick one conditioning set and proceed. Yu et al.\ vary the scoring rule too, but along a different axis, and we know of no controlled estimate of how much the choice of conditioning set matters. We supply one by varying only the protocol with everything else held fixed. The confound in unconditioned rates then takes the form of an identity, $r_{\text{raw}} - r_{\text{cond}} = e\,(1 - r_{\text{cond}})$, which turns a known qualitative caveat into a correction that can be applied directly to published results.

The same design lets us test three readings that architecture comparisons tend to suggest: that CNN gradients are spatially more localized, that adversarial perturbation degrades ViT attention, and that transformer-specific transfer attacks~\cite{zhang2023tgr, naseer2022improving} improve cross-architecture transfer. Only the third is claimed in the cited work; the first two are interpretations. We measure each directly, and none holds in our setting.
