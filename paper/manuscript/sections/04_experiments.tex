\label{subsec:robustness}

The CNN holds an \emph{absolute} robustness advantage under every attack: ResNet-18 AT reaches $37.93\pm0.14\%$ robust accuracy under AutoAttack against $29.14\pm0.40\%$ for ViT-Tiny AT. Because both models of a pair are evaluated on the same 10{,}000 samples with per-sample outcomes logged, McNemar's paired test applies within each run; it favors the CNN decisively in all three pairs ($p < 10^{-84}$ under AutoAttack, $p < 10^{-120}$ under PGD-10). Seed-level variation is small throughout, at most 0.55 points on any reported quantity, so the ordering is not a product of run-to-run noise. The CNN also retains higher clean accuracy ($85.78$ vs $73.53\%$, a 12.3-point gap), reflecting the difficulty of training ViTs on small datasets from scratch; Table~\ref{tab:conditional} separates that contribution from conditional susceptibility. AutoAttack robustness is consistently below PGD-10 robustness for both architectures (6.2 points for ResNet-18, 3.6 for ViT-Tiny), confirming the value of the stronger ensemble. The WideResNet-28-10 reference~\cite{gowal2020uncovering}, at 62.76\%, shows how far capacity, additional training data, and a stronger recipe jointly push robustness beyond the architectural choice; we keep it visually and analytically separate from the matched pair throughout.

Table~\ref{tab:conditional} decomposes the absolute scores into their clean-accuracy and conditional-susceptibility components (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}). Because misclassified clean inputs count as non-robust, the factorization is exact: for AutoAttack it reads $85.78 \times 0.442 = 37.93$ for the CNN and $73.53 \times 0.396 = 29.14$ for the ViT. Both factors favor the CNN. It starts from a 12.3-point higher clean accuracy \emph{and} loses a smaller fraction of what it solves, with conditional fooling of $48.58$ versus $55.53\%$ under PGD-10 and $55.78$ versus $60.37\%$ under AutoAttack. On the jointly solved subset, an exactly paired comparison on shared inputs, the CNN leads by 13.8 and 10.7 points (McNemar $p<10^{-84}$ in every run).

The decomposition is worth reporting even when the two factors agree, because they need not, and because their relative weight is less stable than the ordering they produce. Our first checkpoints, selected with a validation split that clean pre-training had already seen, gave the same absolute ordering but the opposite conditional one: fooling rates that were indistinguishable under PGD-10 ($52.15$ versus $52.33\%$) and slightly ViT-favorable under AutoAttack ($58.16$ versus $56.46\%$), which supported the reading that the CNN advantage was entirely a clean-accuracy effect (lower block of Table~\ref{tab:conditional}). None of the three corrected runs reproduces that reading, and the three agree with one another far more closely than any of them agrees with the earlier run. We cannot isolate a single cause, since the corrected protocol changed the validation handling and the training run at the same time; notably, a simple data-quantity explanation does not hold, because the corrected clean models are \emph{better} than the earlier ones despite seeing 2{,}000 fewer images ($80.09\pm0.60$ versus $77.50\%$ for the ViT). The defensible conclusion is narrower and more useful than a mechanism: an attribution of this kind should not be drawn from a single training run, and a paper that reports only the absolute score hides which factor is doing the work.

\subsection{Transfer Attack Analysis}
\label{subsec:transfer}

This section contains the paper's central measurement. We craft PGD-10 adversarial examples on a source model and evaluate them on a target, on the full test set, for each of the three seed pairs. Because outcomes are logged per sample, the same attack run can be scored under several conditioning protocols without recomputation: the protocols differ only in which samples enter the denominator (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}).

\begin{table}[!t]
\centering
\caption{Transfer asymmetry under the unconditioned rate and three established conditioning protocols (PGD-10, $\eps$=8/255, full test set; mean$\pm$std over three seed pairs). The rightmost column repeats the difference measured on our earlier single-run checkpoints. Denominators (CNN$\rightarrow$ViT / ViT$\rightarrow$CNN): unconditioned 10{,}000/10{,}000; target-correct 7{,}353/8{,}579; both-correct 7{,}061 (shared); successful-source 3{,}122/5{,}331.}
\label{tab:transfer_protocols}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Protocol} & \textbf{CNN$\rightarrow$ViT} & \textbf{ViT$\rightarrow$CNN} & \textbf{Diff.} & \textbf{Diff.}$^{\ddagger}$ \\
\midrule
Unconditioned (raw) & 41.02$\pm$0.55 & 27.45$\pm$0.27 & $+$13.57 & $+$8.27 \\
Target-correct & 19.87$\pm$0.18 & 15.51$\pm$0.59 & $+$4.36 & $+$0.63 \\
Both-correct & 18.25$\pm$0.17 & 9.98$\pm$0.31 & $+$8.27 & $+$5.33 \\
Successful-source & 38.50$\pm$0.75 & 23.91$\pm$0.80 & $+$14.60 & $+$5.28 \\
\bottomrule
\multicolumn{5}{l}{\scriptsize $^{\ddagger}$Single run, validation split seen during clean pre-training.}
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The result is that the protocol, not the model, dominates the measured quantity. On identical models and identical data, the asymmetry ranges from $+4.36\pm0.44$ to $+14.60\pm1.48$ points; the spread between the largest and the smallest protocol estimate is $10.45\pm0.76$ points, a factor of 3.3, whereas the seed-level standard deviation within any single protocol stays below 1.5 points. The protocol also decides the statistical verdict. Under the both-correct protocol, which scores both directions on the same samples and therefore supports paired inference, the difference is $8.27\pm0.24$ points with a paired bootstrap CI of $[7.33, 9.21]$, a sign-flip permutation $p<5\times10^{-5}$ in every seed, and TOST equivalence rejected at every margin we tested; under the target-correct protocol on our earlier checkpoints, the same pipeline accepted equivalence at $\pm$2 points. A reader given only one of these numbers would draw a different conclusion about whether the architectures differ at all.

What is stable is the direction. CNN-crafted examples transfer better to the ViT than the reverse under all four protocols, in all three seeds, and also under the momentum-based MI-FGSM~\cite{dong2018boosting} ($19.07\pm0.33$ versus $15.42\pm0.52\%$ target-correct), so the effect is not an artifact of the PGD attack itself. The magnitude, its significance, and any equivalence claim are protocol-dependent; the sign is not.

The mechanism behind the spread is compositional. Samples that the target solves on clean input but the source does not are three to four times more fragile than jointly solved ones ($41.2\pm0.8\%$ and $59.1\pm1.7\%$ fooling versus $10.0$ and $18.3\%$), and these marginal subsets differ five-fold in size ($1{,}517\pm102$ versus $291\pm16$) because the two models differ in clean accuracy. Conditioning on target-correct admits both marginal subsets, whose asymmetric sizes pull the two rates toward each other; conditioning on both-correct excludes them, and the directional difference reappears.

\subsubsection{How much of the raw rate is the target's clean error?}
\label{subsubsec:matrix}

To quantify the confound directly rather than argue it, we extend the analysis to a $3\times3$ matrix by adding the WideResNet-28-10 reference as both source and target (Table~\ref{tab:transfer_matrix}). Across the six off-diagonal directions, the gap between the unconditioned and the conditioned rate is almost perfectly explained by the clean error of the target: Pearson $r = 0.997$, with a slope of $0.762$ points per point of target error. Unconditioned transfer rates therefore do not measure transferability across architectures; they measure transferability plus the target's clean weakness, in a fixed proportion that we can now state.

The third architecture also reframes the asymmetry itself. Averaged over the two foreign sources, incoming conditioned transfer is $18.50\%$ for the ResNet target, $19.04\%$ for the ViT target, and $10.57\%$ for the WideResNet target, which tracks each target's own white-box conditioned fooling rate ($48.52$, $55.57$ and $25.16\%$) with $r = 0.986$ over the three targets. On this evidence the CNN$\rightarrow$ViT advantage is better read as a property of the weaker target than as evidence that CNN-crafted perturbations are intrinsically more transferable: the most robust target absorbs attacks from both families almost equally well. With only three targets this correlation is suggestive rather than conclusive, and we report it as such.

\subsection{Gradient Characteristics}
\label{subsec:gradient}

To characterize architectural differences in input sensitivity, we analyze the gradient properties of both architectures, together with a CIFAR-native ViT control (32$\times$32 inputs, 4$\times$4 patches, no upsampling) trained with the same adversarial protocol, which isolates the effect of the 32$\rightarrow$224 upsampling in our main ViT pipeline. Table~\ref{tab:gradient} summarizes the key statistics using scale-invariant sparsity measures (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}); gradients are computed with per-sample losses so that norms do not depend on the evaluation batch size.

The gradient analysis reveals consistent but modest architectural differences. Across all three scale-invariant sparsity measures, CNN gradients are sparser than ViT gradients (Hoyer 0.474 vs 0.449; Gini 0.634 vs 0.606; 31.5\% vs 25.8\% of components below 1\% of the per-sample maximum), indicating that CNN sensitivity is somewhat more concentrated in fewer input components. Because the same 500 samples are measured under both models, we test these differences with per-sample paired statistics: all three are highly significant (Wilcoxon signed-rank, Holm-corrected $p < 10^{-15}$) with small-to-medium paired effect sizes (Cohen's $d$ of 0.34, 0.65, and 0.43 for Hoyer, Gini, and the relative-threshold fraction), quantifying ``consistent but modest'' precisely. Interestingly, this ordering is a product of adversarial training: in the clean-trained counterparts the ViT is the sparser model (Hoyer 0.406 vs 0.354), and adversarial training sparsifies the CNN far more strongly ($+$0.12 Hoyer) than the ViT ($+$0.04), consistent with the known sparsifying effect of adversarial training on input gradients~\cite{chalasani2020concise}. The native-resolution ViT control is \emph{less} sparse than the upsampled ViT on every measure, so the CNN--ViT sparsity ordering is not an artifact of the 32$\rightarrow$224 upsampling---if anything, upsampling moves the ViT \emph{toward} the CNN. We emphasize that these differences are far smaller than a threshold-based sparsity metric would suggest: an earlier version of this analysis (fixed $10^{-6}$ threshold, measured on the preliminary run2 models) indicated a 1.7$\times$ sparsity ratio, most of which reflected gradient-scale differences rather than structure; on the final models the same scale-dependent metric inflates the ratio even further ($\approx$2.4$\times$). Cross-sample gradient alignment is low for both architectures but measurably higher for the ViT (0.052 vs 0.038 in mean absolute cosine over all pairs, a 1.36$\times$ ratio; native control 0.079), suggesting that transformer sensitivity axes are somewhat more shared across inputs. The \emph{signed} mean cosine, by contrast, is near zero for every model ($\leq$0.002): what is shared across samples is sensitivity axes, not consistent directions, so the alignment gap should not be read as direct evidence of cheap universal perturbations. We treat both characteristics as behavioral correlates of, rather than established explanations for, the architectures' differing adversarial behavior.

\subsection{Feature Degradation in Vision Transformers}
\label{subsec:attention}

We analyze how adversarial perturbations affect ViT feature representations across layers, extending layer-wise representation analyses of clean-trained ViTs~\cite{raghu2021vision} to the adversarially trained setting. The measured representations are the outputs of each transformer block's MLP sub-block; the attention-weight analysis accompanies the figure-level results. Table~\ref{tab:feature_degradation} shows all twelve blocks ($n$=100 test samples).

The layer-wise analysis reveals a characteristic degradation profile. Cosine similarity between clean and adversarial features drops quickly in the early blocks (0.994 at Block~0 to 0.972 by Block~4), reaches its minimum in the middle-to-late network (0.955 at Block~8), and then plateaus with a slight recovery in the final blocks (0.965 at Blocks~10--11). Most of the directional deviation is therefore accumulated by mid-network rather than growing monotonically to the output---an ``early-drop, mid-network-plateau'' profile rather than strictly progressive degradation. The final-block recovery is statistically reliable at the batch level (Block~8 vs Block~11 cosine similarity, Welch $t=-4.07$, $p=0.005$). A parallel analysis of the adversarially trained ResNet-18's eight residual-block outputs (same attack, same metrics, $n$=100) shows a qualitatively different profile: cosine similarity degrades \emph{monotonically} with depth (0.995 at layer1.0 down to 0.913 at layer4.1, with no plateau or recovery), accompanied by much larger norm distortions in the final blocks ($-$6.4\% at layer4.0, $+$7.2\% at layer4.1). The plateau-and-recovery shape with preserved norms is therefore specific to the evaluated ViT in our model pair, not a generic property of deep networks under attack. Feature norm changes remain modest throughout (at most $-$1.4\%), indicating that adversarial perturbations corrupt the \emph{direction} of representations far more than their magnitude. One untested hypothesis is that the mild recovery in the final blocks reflects a regularizing influence of the classifier head on the last transformer blocks. Even so, the terminal deviation (cosine similarity $\approx$0.965) is sufficient to flip predictions on most attacked samples: on these 100 samples, accuracy drops from 79\% clean to 39\% under PGD-10.

\label{fig:feature_degradation}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:feature_degradation} complements the feature-level analysis with attention-level measurements. Notably, the per-layer \emph{entropy} of the CLS attention distribution is nearly unchanged under attack (mean absolute change $\approx$0.015 nats on layer means of $\approx$4.9 nats, at most 0.8\%; measured on the 8-sample figure batch): the attack redirects \emph{where} attention goes (Figure~\ref{fig:attention_layers}) without broadening or sharpening its distribution, reinforcing that the operative degradation is directional drift in the feature space rather than attention diffusion.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../figures/final/fig_tsne_features.pdf}
\caption{Illustrative t-SNE embedding of penultimate features for clean samples (circles) and white-box PGD-10 adversarial samples (triangles), for ResNet-18 (left) and ViT-Tiny (right). t-SNE is shown for orientation only; the quantitative comparison is given in the text.}
\label{fig:tsne_features}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:tsne_features} shows an illustrative t-SNE embedding of the penultimate feature spaces; because t-SNE embeddings are sensitive to hyperparameters, we base all claims on quantitative metrics computed in the original feature space ($n$=500, white-box PGD-10 per model). These metrics show that the attack collapses class structure most strongly in the \emph{CNN}: clean-fit 5-NN label consistency drops from 85.2\% to 47.8\%, the silhouette score falls from 0.14 to $-$0.06, the intra/inter-class distance ratio rises from 0.64 to 0.91, and the relative class-centroid shift is 0.29. The ViT's penultimate space, by contrast, is only weakly class-separated even on clean inputs (silhouette $\approx$0; 5-NN consistency 68.2\%, dropping to 53.4\% under attack) and its centroids shift far less (0.09). An earlier qualitative reading of these plots (``more class-coherent CNN displacement, greater ViT inter-class confusion'') is not supported by the quantitative metrics and we do not maintain it; the measurable statement is that the CNN's cleanly well-separated feature space is heavily disrupted by white-box attack, whereas the ViT's feature-space class structure is weaker to begin with and degrades less in these aggregate terms.

\subsection{Statistical Validation}

To quantify evaluation stochasticity, we evaluate each trained model across 3 different random seeds (42, 123, 456) for attack initialization on 1,000 test samples. Table~\ref{tab:statistical} shows the results.

\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Clean Acc (\%)} & \textbf{PGD-10 Acc (\%)} \\
\midrule
ResNet-18 AT & 86.10 $\pm$ 0.00 & 40.57 $\pm$ 0.12 \\

The low variance confirms that evaluation stochasticity is negligible. The evaluated subset and its ordering are identical across seeds (the test loader does not shuffle), so clean accuracy shows exactly zero variance and the only stochastic element is the random initialization of PGD restarts. Robust accuracy shows minimal variation (standard deviation 0.12 percentage points for CNN, 0.09 for ViT), indicating stable attack behavior across random restarts. This procedure quantifies attack-initialization stochasticity only; it does not measure data-sampling or training-level variance. (Differences between Table~\ref{tab:statistical} and Table~\ref{tab:main_results} PGD values---0.4 points for the CNN and up to 2.1 points for the ViT---reflect the easier first-1{,}000-sample subset versus the full test set.)

Beyond evaluation seeds, we conducted multiple independent adversarial-training runs per architecture during this study. Under the final training protocol, ResNet-18 AT reached 40.97\% and ViT-Tiny AT 36.09\% full-test PGD-10 accuracy; earlier runs under a preliminary implementation of the training loop (differing in attack-generation details, later standardized as described in Section~\ref{subsec:defense_methods}) produced values in the 40.3--41.0\% (CNN) and 32.8--36.9\% (ViT) ranges. The CNN-over-ViT ranking was preserved in every run, though the ViT's run-to-run variation is clearly larger than evaluation-level variance, and we therefore avoid attributing precise magnitudes to the architecture gap beyond the paired analysis of Section~\ref{subsec:robustness}.

\textbf{Computational Details:} All experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 5060 Ti (16GB VRAM) using PyTorch 2.6.0 with CUDA 12.8. AutoAttack evaluation used the standard configuration (APGD-CE, APGD-T, FAB-T, Square) on the full 10{,}000-sample test set, evaluated in seeded 1{,}000-sample chunks with per-sample outcome logging; total wall-clock time was several hours per model on this hardware.
