\subsection{Robustness Comparison}
\label{subsec:robustness}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} presents the main robustness comparison across all evaluated models. We report clean accuracy and robust accuracy under PGD-10 and AutoAttack with $\eps = 8/255$.

\begin{table}[!t]
\centering

Checkpoints were selected on a held-out validation split (Section~\ref{subsec:training}). AA = AutoAttack (standard suite).}
\label{tab:main_results}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{2.5pt}
\begin{tabular}{lcccccc}

\multicolumn{7}{l}{\textit{CNN Models}} \\

\midrule
\multicolumn{7}{l}{\textit{Vision Transformer Models}} \\

\multicolumn{7}{l}{\scriptsize \phantom{$^\dagger$}RobustBench-reported figure (we did not rerun AutoAttack on it).}
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The results indicate a statistically significant robustness advantage for the CNN under standard adversarial training: on the full test set, ResNet-18 AT achieves 35.74\% robust accuracy under AutoAttack, compared to 32.94\% for ViT-Tiny AT---a 2.80 percentage-point gap. Because both models are evaluated on the same 10{,}000 samples with per-sample outcomes logged, we can apply McNemar's paired test: 940 samples are robustly classified only by the CNN versus 660 only by the ViT ($\chi^2 = 48.65$, $p < 10^{-11}$), so the gap is not attributable to evaluation noise. The 95\% binomial confidence half-widths are below $\pm$1 percentage point per model. Two caveats remain. First, the gap is modest relative to training-level variability: across our training runs the ViT's PGD-10 accuracy varied by roughly four points (Section~\ref{subsec:statistics_results}), so the architecture comparison should be read as ``under matched training recipes'' rather than as a universal constant. Second, the CNN also retains a higher clean accuracy (85.42\% vs 75.65\%, a 9.8-point gap), reflecting the difficulty of training ViTs on small datasets from scratch. AutoAttack robustness is consistently lower than PGD-10 robustness for both architectures (5.17 points for ResNet-18, 3.05 points for ViT-Tiny), confirming the importance of using this stronger benchmark. The WideResNet-28-10 baseline~\cite{gowal2020uncovering}, at 62.76\% robust accuracy, demonstrates how far capacity, additional training data, and a stronger training recipe jointly push robustness beyond architectural choice alone.

\subsection{Transfer Attack Analysis}
\label{subsec:transfer}

We analyze the transferability of adversarial examples between architectures using PGD-10 attacks on the full test set ($n$=10{,}000). Table~\ref{tab:transfer} reports the conditioned fooling rate defined in Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}---the fraction of samples the target classifies correctly on clean inputs that become misclassified under adversarial examples crafted on the source---with raw misclassification rates in parentheses for transparency.

\begin{table}[!t]
\centering
\caption{Transfer Attack Matrix on the full test set ($n$=10{,}000). Cells show the conditioned fooling rate with 95\% bootstrap confidence intervals (10{,}000 resamples); raw target-misclassification rates in parentheses. Diagonal entries are white-box attacks.}
\label{tab:transfer}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Source} $\rightarrow$ \textbf{Target} & \textbf{ResNet-18 AT} & \textbf{ViT-Tiny AT} \\
\midrule
ResNet-18 AT & 52.2 [51.1, 53.3] (59.1) & 21.0 [20.0, 21.9] (40.2) \\
ViT-Tiny AT & 20.3 [19.5, 21.2] (31.9) & 52.3 [51.3, 53.4] (63.9) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The conditioned analysis yields two findings. First, once the targets' unequal clean accuracy is controlled for, cross-architecture transfer between this model pair (under PGD-10 at $\eps=8/255$) is \emph{symmetric}: CNN-to-ViT fooling is 20.95\% [20.03, 21.88] and ViT-to-CNN fooling is 20.32\% [19.47, 21.19], a 0.63-point difference that is not significant under a two-proportion test ($z=0.98$, $p=0.33$) and, more stringently, is formally \emph{equivalent} within a $\pm$2-point margin under a two one-sided test (TOST, $p=0.016$). Second, the raw rates paint a misleadingly different picture (40.2\% vs 31.9\%, an apparent 8.3-point asymmetry favoring CNN sources): because the ViT target has roughly ten points more clean error than the CNN target (75.65\% vs 85.42\% clean accuracy), the raw metric credits the CNN source with failures the ViT would have made on clean inputs anyway. This is a cautionary methodological result for cross-architecture transfer studies: apparent transfer asymmetries between targets of unequal clean accuracy can be manufactured entirely by the metric. Notably, the white-box conditioned fooling rates are almost identical across architectures (52.2\% vs 52.3\%), indicating that---under matched adversarial training---the two architectures are comparably vulnerable on the samples they classify correctly.

\subsection{Gradient Characteristics}
\label{subsec:gradient}

To characterize architectural differences in input sensitivity, we analyze the gradient properties of both architectures, together with a CIFAR-native ViT control (32$\times$32 inputs, 4$\times$4 patches, no upsampling) trained with the same adversarial protocol, which isolates the effect of the 32$\rightarrow$224 upsampling in our main ViT pipeline. Table~\ref{tab:gradient} summarizes the key statistics using scale-invariant sparsity measures (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}); gradients are computed with per-sample losses so that norms do not depend on the evaluation batch size.

\begin{table}[!t]
\centering
\caption{Gradient Characteristics ($n$=500 test samples; per-sample loss gradients; alignment is the all-pairs mean absolute cosine similarity within batches of 50; $\pm$ values are std across 10 batch means). All ResNet--ViT differences on the scale-invariant metrics are significant at the batch level (Welch $t$-test, $p < 3 \times 10^{-5}$).}
\label{tab:gradient}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{2.5pt}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Metric} & \textbf{ResNet-18 AT} & \textbf{ViT-Tiny AT} & \textbf{ViT (native)$^\ddagger$} \\
\midrule
$L_2$ Norm (mean) & 0.698 & 0.760 & 0.554 \\
$L_\infty$ Norm (mean) & 0.139 & 0.144 & 0.087 \\
Hoyer Sparsity & \textbf{0.474}$\pm$0.009 & 0.449$\pm$0.011 & 0.405$\pm$0.008 \\
Gini Coefficient & \textbf{0.634}$\pm$0.006 & 0.606$\pm$0.008 & 0.571$\pm$0.006 \\
Frac.\ $<$1\% of max & \textbf{31.5\%}$\pm$1.7 & 25.8\%$\pm$1.7 & 17.9\%$\pm$1.2 \\
Gradient Alignment & 0.038$\pm$0.002 & \textbf{0.052}$\pm$0.002 & 0.079$\pm$0.005 \\
\bottomrule
\multicolumn{4}{l}{\scriptsize $^\ddagger$CIFAR-native ViT control (4$\times$4 patches, no upsampling; 5.4M}\\
\multicolumn{4}{l}{\scriptsize \phantom{$^\ddagger$}params; checkpoint-selection clean/robust acc.\ 62.4\%/31.8\%).}\\
\multicolumn{4}{l}{\scriptsize \phantom{$^\ddagger$}Included only to test the upsampling confound.}
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The gradient analysis reveals consistent but modest architectural differences. Across all three scale-invariant sparsity measures, CNN gradients are sparser than ViT gradients (Hoyer 0.474 vs 0.449; Gini 0.634 vs 0.606; 31.5\% vs 25.8\% of components below 1\% of the per-sample maximum), indicating that CNN sensitivity is somewhat more concentrated in localized regions. The native-resolution ViT control is \emph{less} sparse than the upsampled ViT on every measure, so the CNN--ViT sparsity ordering is not an artifact of the 32$\rightarrow$224 upsampling---if anything, upsampling moves the ViT \emph{toward} the CNN. We emphasize that these differences are far smaller than a threshold-based sparsity metric would suggest: an earlier version of this analysis (fixed $10^{-6}$ threshold, measured on the preliminary run2 models) indicated a 1.7$\times$ sparsity ratio, most of which reflected gradient-scale differences rather than structure; on the final models the same scale-dependent metric inflates the ratio even further ($\approx$2.4$\times$). Cross-sample gradient alignment is low for both architectures but measurably higher for the ViT (0.052 vs 0.038, a 1.36$\times$ ratio; native control 0.079), suggesting that transformer sensitivity directions are somewhat more shared across inputs. We treat both characteristics as behavioral correlates of, rather than established explanations for, the architectures' differing adversarial behavior.

\subsection{Feature Degradation in Vision Transformers}
\label{subsec:attention}

We analyze how adversarial perturbations affect ViT feature representations across layers, extending layer-wise representation analyses of clean-trained ViTs~\cite{raghu2021vision} to the adversarially trained setting. The measured representations are the outputs of each transformer block's MLP sub-block; the attention-weight analysis accompanies the figure-level results. Table~\ref{tab:feature_degradation} shows all twelve blocks ($n$=100 test samples).

\begin{table}[!t]
\centering
\caption{ViT-Tiny Feature Degradation Under PGD-10 Attack ($n$=100 test samples; features are MLP sub-block outputs; $\pm$ values are std across 5 batch means)}
\label{tab:feature_degradation}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Layer} & \textbf{$L_2$ Distance} & \textbf{Cosine Sim.} & \textbf{Norm Change} \\
\midrule
Block 0 & 2.73 $\pm$ 0.13 & 0.994 $\pm$ 0.000 & $-$0.11 $\pm$ 0.17\% \\
Block 1 & 1.74 $\pm$ 0.07 & 0.992 $\pm$ 0.001 & $-$0.05 $\pm$ 0.22\% \\
Block 2 & 1.81 $\pm$ 0.05 & 0.981 $\pm$ 0.002 & $-$0.99 $\pm$ 0.31\% \\
Block 3 & 1.33 $\pm$ 0.05 & 0.974 $\pm$ 0.002 & $-$0.69 $\pm$ 1.24\% \\
Block 4 & 1.71 $\pm$ 0.08 & 0.972 $\pm$ 0.002 & $-$1.07 $\pm$ 1.76\% \\
Block 5 & 1.92 $\pm$ 0.03 & 0.972 $\pm$ 0.003 & $-$0.29 $\pm$ 1.54\% \\
Block 6 & 1.84 $\pm$ 0.04 & 0.961 $\pm$ 0.004 & $-$0.28 $\pm$ 0.83\% \\
Block 7 & 1.48 $\pm$ 0.06 & 0.963 $\pm$ 0.004 & $-$1.40 $\pm$ 0.99\% \\
Block 8 & 1.79 $\pm$ 0.03 & \textbf{0.955} $\pm$ 0.003 & $-$1.05 $\pm$ 0.27\% \\
Block 9 & 1.86 $\pm$ 0.02 & 0.963 $\pm$ 0.002 & $-$0.73 $\pm$ 0.54\% \\
Block 10 & 1.73 $\pm$ 0.09 & 0.965 $\pm$ 0.003 & $-$0.76 $\pm$ 0.45\% \\
Block 11 & 2.31 $\pm$ 0.15 & 0.965 $\pm$ 0.004 & $-$1.11 $\pm$ 0.80\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The layer-wise analysis reveals a characteristic degradation profile. Cosine similarity between clean and adversarial features drops quickly in the early blocks (0.994 at Block~0 to 0.972 by Block~4), reaches its minimum in the middle-to-late network (0.955 at Block~8), and then plateaus with a slight recovery in the final blocks (0.965 at Blocks~10--11). Most of the directional deviation is therefore accumulated by mid-network rather than growing monotonically to the output---an ``early-drop, mid-network-plateau'' profile rather than strictly progressive degradation. The final-block recovery is statistically reliable at the batch level (Block~8 vs Block~11 cosine similarity, Welch $t=-4.07$, $p=0.005$). Feature norm changes remain modest throughout (at most $-$1.4\%), indicating that adversarial perturbations corrupt the \emph{direction} of representations far more than their magnitude. The mild recovery in the final blocks may reflect the regularizing influence of the classifier head on the last transformer blocks. Even so, the terminal deviation (cosine similarity $\approx$0.965) is sufficient to flip predictions on most attacked samples: on these 100 samples, accuracy drops from 79\% clean to 39\% under PGD-10. A limitation of this analysis is the absence of a comparable layer-wise study for CNN, which would help establish whether this profile is ViT-specific or a general property of deep networks under adversarial attack.

\subsection{Statistical Validation}
\label{subsec:statistics_results}

To quantify evaluation stochasticity, we evaluate each trained model across 3 different random seeds (42, 123, 456) for attack initialization on 1,000 test samples. Table~\ref{tab:statistical} shows the results.

\begin{table}[!t]
\centering
\caption{Statistical Validation (Mean $\pm$ Std over 3 evaluation seeds, first 1{,}000 test samples)}
\label{tab:statistical}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Clean Acc (\%)} & \textbf{PGD-10 Acc (\%)} \\
\midrule
ResNet-18 AT & 86.10 $\pm$ 0.00 & 40.57 $\pm$ 0.12 \\
ViT-Tiny AT & 76.90 $\pm$ 0.00 & 38.23 $\pm$ 0.09 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The low variance confirms that evaluation stochasticity is negligible. The evaluated subset and its ordering are identical across seeds (the test loader does not shuffle), so clean accuracy shows exactly zero variance and the only stochastic element is the random initialization of PGD restarts. Robust accuracy shows minimal variation (standard deviation 0.12 percentage points for CNN, 0.09 for ViT), indicating stable attack behavior across random restarts. This procedure quantifies attack-initialization stochasticity only; it does not measure data-sampling or training-level variance. (Differences between Table~\ref{tab:statistical} and Table~\ref{tab:main_results} PGD values---0.3 points for the CNN and up to 2.2 points for the ViT---reflect the easier first-1{,}000-sample subset versus the full test set.)

Beyond evaluation seeds, we conducted multiple independent adversarial-training runs per architecture during this study. Under the final training protocol, ResNet-18 AT reached 40.91\% and ViT-Tiny AT 35.99\% full-test PGD-10 accuracy; earlier runs under a preliminary implementation of the training loop (differing in attack-generation details, later standardized as described in Section~\ref{subsec:defense_methods}) produced values in the 40.3--41.0\% (CNN) and 32.8--36.9\% (ViT) ranges. The CNN-over-ViT ranking was preserved in every run, though the ViT's run-to-run variation is clearly larger than evaluation-level variance, and we therefore avoid attributing precise magnitudes to the architecture gap beyond the paired analysis of Section~\ref{subsec:robustness}.

\textbf{Computational Details:} All experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 5060 Ti (16GB VRAM) using PyTorch 2.6.0 with CUDA 12.8. AutoAttack evaluation used the standard configuration (APGD-CE, APGD-T, FAB-T, Square) on the full 10{,}000-sample test set, evaluated in seeded 1{,}000-sample chunks with per-sample outcome logging; total wall-clock time was several hours per model on this hardware.
