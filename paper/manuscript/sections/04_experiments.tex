The CNN holds an \emph{absolute} robustness advantage under every attack: ResNet-18 AT reaches $37.93\pm0.14\%$ robust accuracy under AutoAttack against $29.14\pm0.40\%$ for ViT-Tiny AT. Because both models of a pair are evaluated on the same 10{,}000 samples with per-sample outcomes logged, McNemar's paired test applies within each run and favors the CNN in all three pairs ($p < 2\times10^{-84}$ under AutoAttack, $p < 2\times10^{-120}$ under PGD-10). Seed-level variation for the adversarially trained pair is at most 0.55 points on any reported accuracy, so the ordering is not run-to-run noise. The CNN also retains higher clean accuracy ($85.78$ vs $73.53\%$, a 12.3-point gap), reflecting the difficulty of training ViTs on small datasets from scratch, and AutoAttack falls below PGD-10 for both architectures (6.2 points for ResNet-18, 3.6 for ViT-Tiny). The WideResNet-28-10 reference~\cite{gowal2020uncovering}, at 62.76\%, shows how far capacity, extra training data and a stronger recipe jointly push robustness beyond the architectural choice; we keep it separate from the matched pair throughout.

Table~\ref{tab:conditional} decomposes the absolute scores into their clean-accuracy and conditional-susceptibility components (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}). Because misclassified clean inputs count as non-robust, the factorization is exact: for AutoAttack it reads $85.78 \times 0.442 = 37.93$ for the CNN and $73.53 \times 0.396 = 29.14$ for the ViT. Both factors favor the CNN. It starts from a 12.3-point higher clean accuracy \emph{and} loses a smaller fraction of what it solves, with conditional fooling of $48.58$ versus $55.53\%$ under PGD-10 and $55.78$ versus $60.37\%$ under AutoAttack. On the $n=7{,}061\pm68$ samples both models classify correctly on clean inputs, an exactly paired comparison, the CNN leads by 13.8 and 10.7 points (McNemar $p<10^{-67}$ in every run; the bound is computed on this subset and is weaker than the full-test-set bound quoted above, which is what the larger denominator buys).

The denominators differ sharply between protocols. For CNN$\rightarrow$ViT and ViT$\rightarrow$CNN respectively they are 10{,}000/10{,}000 without conditioning, 7{,}353/8{,}579 under target-correct, 7{,}061 shared samples under both-correct, and 2{,}831/3{,}814 under successful-source. On identical models and identical data the asymmetry ranges from $+4.36\pm0.44$ to $+19.37\pm1.27$ points, a 4.4-fold ratio between the extremes, and the mean per-pair spread between the largest and the smallest protocol estimate is $15.01\pm0.84$ points. % REF: Turetilmis, Protokol farklarinin farki (raw-tc +9.21+-0.76; ss-raw +5.79; ss-bc +11.10+-1.09; bc-tc +3.91+-0.32;

The spread is not a single quantity. On CIFAR-10 it splits into two distances between adjacent protocols (Figure~\ref{fig:protocol_sets}b). The successful-source estimate conditions on the source's own white-box failure and therefore estimates a different quantity, how well an attack that already worked carries over; it lies $11.10\pm1.09$ points above the both-correct estimate. The two protocols we endorse for directional claims, target-correct and both-correct, which differ only in whether the source must also be correct, are $3.91\pm0.32$ points apart, with a run-level 95\% $t$ interval of $[3.12, 4.70]$ (two degrees of freedom); that distance is the difference between two estimands, not analyst discretion, and the marginal subsets described below account for it. Because target-correct is the lowest and successful-source the highest estimate in every pair, the two distances make up the $15.01$-point range. The unconditioned estimate lies inside it, $9.21\pm0.76$ points above target-correct and $5.79$ points below successful-source, which is where Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_identity} places it given the targets' clean errors, the conditioned rates and the measured premise $p$ (Section~\ref{subsubsec:matrix}). The endorsed distance is $5.97$ points on CIFAR-100 (interval $[3.53, 8.40]$), $1.95$ points under the $L_2$ budget ($[1.33, 2.56]$), and $0.82$ and $0.49$ points in the two SVHN pairs. On CIFAR-100 the unconditioned estimate is the upper end of the range, and the identity term, $13.58$ points, makes up nearly all of the $13.83$-point spread.

The protocol also changes the statistical reading. Under the both-correct protocol, which scores both directions on the same samples and therefore supports paired inference, the difference is $8.27\pm0.24$ points, differing slightly from the $\pm0.23$ of the table only because the two estimators differ. Each run has its own paired bootstrap CI ($[7.61, 9.44]$, $[7.10, 9.01]$ and $[7.28, 9.20]$). Averaging their endpoints gives $[7.33, 9.22]$, a description of three intervals that carries no coverage guarantee of its own; for inference at the run level, the across-run $t$ interval is $[7.68, 8.86]$. The sign-flip permutation $p$ is below $5\times10^{-5}$ in every seed, and TOST does not establish equivalence at any of the three margins ($\pm1$, $\pm2$ and $\pm3$ points), since every interval lies entirely outside $\pm3$.

% REF: Turetilmis, Basarili-kaynak: siki ve gevsek okuma (+14.60; 10.45; +4.77+-0.39)
Protocol names also leave sub-choices open. Under the loose reading of successful-source (Table~\ref{tab:protocol_masks}), which also admits samples the source already misclassifies on clean input, the measured asymmetry falls from $+19.37$ to $+14.60$ points and the protocol spread from $15.01$ to $10.45$ points. That shift of $4.77\pm0.39$ points is larger than the run-to-run standard deviation of any protocol in Table~\ref{tab:transfer_protocols} by more than a factor of three, and the other three protocols are unchanged by construction.

What is stable on CIFAR-10 is the direction: CNN-crafted examples transfer better to the ViT than the reverse under all four protocols, in all three seeds, and also under the momentum-based MI-FGSM~\cite{dong2018boosting} ($19.07\pm0.33$ versus $15.42\pm0.52\%$ target-correct; this bidirectional check uses the attack's own default step of $\alpha=\eps/10$, not the $\alpha=2/255$ used elsewhere, which is why its ViT$\rightarrow$CNN figure differs slightly from the matched-budget value in Table~\ref{tab:tgr}), so the effect is not an artifact of the PGD attack itself. Its magnitude and significance, and with them any equivalence claim, depend on the protocol.

% REF: Govde metnindeki diger sayilar, M8.5 (marjinal alt kumeler 1517+-102 / 291+-16; 41.2 / 59.1 / 10.0 / 18.2)
The distance between the two endorsed protocols has a compositional source. Samples that the target solves on clean input but the source does not are three to four times more fragile than jointly solved ones ($41.2\pm0.8\%$ and $59.1\pm1.7\%$ fooling versus $10.0$ and $18.2\%$), and these marginal subsets differ five-fold in size ($1{,}517\pm102$ versus $291\pm16$) because the two models differ in clean accuracy. Conditioning on target-correct admits both marginal subsets, whose asymmetric sizes pull the two rates toward each other; conditioning on both-correct excludes them, and the directional difference reappears. This account covers three of the four protocols. The successful-source protocol selects by the \emph{source's} own robustness, which is not a function of the pair's clean-accuracy gap, so it contributes a second and partly opposing term (Section~\ref{subsubsec:spread_gap}).

\subsubsection{How much of the raw rate is the target's clean error?}
\label{subsubsec:matrix}

% REF: C3: WRN dahil 3x3 matris (r = 0.997; egim 0.762)
% REF: Dis veri: Mahmood, "Ozdeslikte kullanilan p" (p_min 0.989014, results/q1/ozdeslik_kontrol.json)
Adding the WideResNet-28-10 reference as both source and target gives a $3\times3$ matrix for each training pair (Table~\ref{tab:transfer_matrix}), with six off-diagonal directions in which Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_identity} can be checked against targets of very different clean error. Its premise holds tightly on these adversarially trained targets: across $36$ off-diagonal directions (two datasets, three seed pairs, three targets, two sources) the measured $p$ ranges from $0.989$ to $1.000$. The $p=1$ form therefore reproduces every measured unconditioned rate to within $0.41$ points, with a mean absolute residual of $0.095$ points and at most $1.39\%$ of the deviation it explains. That residual is $e\,(1-p)$, the premise restated in points, and not a second confirmation; the informative quantity is $p$ itself. Stated as a correlation between the deviation and the target's clean error ($r = 0.997$), the same result would be understated: a near-unit correlation between a quantity and its own dominant component is arithmetic.

What remains dataset-specific is the proportionality factor. Because $(1 - r_{\text{cond}})$ depends on how transferable the attack is, the fitted slope is $0.762$ points per point of target error on CIFAR-10 and $0.656$ on CIFAR-100, an average over the targets measured that should be re-estimated whenever the identity is applied to other model pools. Unconditioned transfer rates therefore do not measure transferability across architectures; they measure transferability plus the target's clean weakness, in a proportion that Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_identity} makes explicit.

\subsection{Source Effect, Not Target Weakness}
\label{subsec:source_target}

The $3\times3$ matrices also say where the direction comes from. A CNN$\rightarrow$ViT advantage can arise because CNN-crafted examples transfer better to any target, a source effect, or because the ViT is the easier target for any source, a target effect. We separate the two with an additive model fitted, protocol by protocol, to the six off-diagonal rates of each training pair, $r(s\rightarrow t) = \mu + \alpha_s + \beta_t + \varepsilon_{st}$ with sum-to-zero coding, so that the asymmetry equals the source difference $\alpha_{\text{CNN}} - \alpha_{\text{ViT}}$ plus the target difference $\beta_{\text{ViT}} - \beta_{\text{CNN}}$ plus a residual difference. Six cells and five parameters leave one residual degree of freedom, so a high $R^2$ is expected and carries little evidence: the fit describes each matrix and tests nothing, and the fixed-third-model contrasts below need no model at all.

% REF: Kaynak-hedef toplamsal ayristirma, CIFAR-10 ve CIFAR-100 cift basina ayristirma
%      (tc: +5.13+-0.09 / -2.03+-0.51 / +4.41+-0.45; C100 +9.54+-0.64 / -6.19+-0.93;
%       ham: hedef farki +8.13+-0.33, C100 +10.72+-0.97)
% REF: Kaynak-hedef toplamsal ayristirma, CIFAR-10 uc tohum ortalamasi matris (WRN hedefine 12.48 / 8.65; WRN kaynagindan 21.48 / 18.15)
% REF: C3: WRN dahil 3x3 matris (gelen transfer 18.5 / 19.04 / 10.57; r = 0.986)
The source term carries the asymmetry on both datasets (Table~\ref{tab:source_target}). On CIFAR-10 the source difference is $+5.13\pm0.09$ points and the target difference $-2.03\pm0.51$: examples crafted on the ViT transfer worse, and the ViT is the \emph{less} vulnerable of the two targets, so the target term works against the measured asymmetry of $+4.41\pm0.45$. CIFAR-100 repeats the pattern at a larger size ($+9.54\pm0.64$ against $-6.19\pm0.93$). The source term keeps its sign under every protocol we can score, whereas the target term does not. Under both-correct the target difference is $-0.53\pm0.59$ on CIFAR-10, indistinguishable from zero, and $-2.85\pm0.93$ on CIFAR-100, while the source difference grows to $+6.76\pm0.35$ and $+11.79\pm0.83$; under successful-source, which the CIFAR-100 records allow, the terms are $+20.82\pm1.43$ and $-8.12\pm2.23$. The WideResNet supplies the same contrasts without a model. As a fixed target it is fooled by $12.48\%$ of ResNet-crafted and $8.65\%$ of ViT-crafted examples, so ViT-crafted examples transfer worse even to a third architecture; as a fixed source it fools the ResNet at $21.48\%$ and the ViT at $18.15\%$, so the ViT is also the harder target for a third model's perturbations. The fixed-model contrasts keep these signs under both-correct ($+4.69\pm0.38$ and $-2.60\pm0.72$ on CIFAR-10), and the target contrast is negative in every conditioned protocol on both datasets. Scored on raw rates, the same decomposition gives a target difference of $+8.13$ points on CIFAR-10 and $+10.72$ on CIFAR-100: unconditioned rates count the ViT's larger clean error and make it look like the weaker target. A weaker-target reading cannot rest on the incoming-transfer correlation either. Incoming conditioned transfer does track each target's own white-box conditioned fooling rate ($r = 0.986$ over the three targets), but that correlation is carried by the robust WideResNet, which absorbs attacks from both families ($10.57\%$ incoming); between the ResNet and the ViT, incoming transfer differs by $0.54$ points ($18.50$ against $19.04\%$) while the asymmetry is $4.41$.

%      (R2 0.3681 / 0.4508; aile-terimli 0.7852 / 0.8425; CNN_vs_ViT_hibrit_haric -25.01 / -10.78;
%       ResNet_vs_ViT C100: -21.90 / +30.15, aile-terimli -8.55 / +16.81)
% REF: Dis veri: Mahmood ve ark., Aile duzeyi asimetri ve guclu kaynak ailesi sayimi (12/12; 6/6)
% REF: Dis veri: Mahmood ve ark., Rejim notlari (m = 1000, FGSM/PGD/MIM en buyugu, eps 0.031, ImageNet-21K)
The $8\times8$ matrices of Mahmood et al.~\cite{mahmood2021robustness} admit the same decomposition on models trained elsewhere, under their both-correct protocol with $m=1000$ samples per pair and the strongest of FGSM, PGD and MIM in each cell ($\eps=0.031$). Their regime differs from ours in every respect that matters here: the ViT and BiT-M models are pre-trained on ImageNet-21k and fine-tuned without adversarial training, ResNet-56 and ResNet-164 are trained on CIFAR without adversarial training, and their diagonal is an independently trained copy of the same model, not a white-box attack, so we exclude it. The additive model fits their matrices poorly ($R^2=0.368$ on CIFAR-10 and $0.451$ on CIFAR-100) because transfer within a family is much stronger than across families; a same-family term raises $R^2$ to $0.785$ and $0.843$. Under either fit the family-level source difference favors the ViT on CIFAR-10 ($-25.01$ points additive, $-10.78$ with the family term, CNN minus ViT), the opposite of ours, and the ViT is the stronger source in all $12$ cross-family pairs that exclude the hybrid ViT-R50. On CIFAR-100 their ResNet-56 and ResNet-164 show our direction, CNN$\rightarrow$ViT stronger in $6/6$ pairs, but the target term carries it ($+30.15$ additive, $+16.81$ with the family term) while the source term still favors the ViT ($-21.90$ and $-8.55$). The direction agrees with ours there; the mechanism does not. Section~\ref{subsec:findings} discusses what this leaves open.

\subsubsection{A second dataset: CIFAR-100}
\label{subsec:cifar100}

A protocol effect that exists only on CIFAR-10 would not support a general reporting recommendation, so we repeated the whole design on CIFAR-100: same architectures, same threat model, same attack budget, same three-seed structure, same four protocols. Three directional predictions and three endpoint bands were registered in an append-only document before any measurement from this dataset existed, so that the outcome could not be reinterpreted after the fact. The registration does not precede the start of training; its commit precedes the first reported number, an epoch-5 pilot gate, by sixty-six minutes.

Absolute robustness is lower than on CIFAR-10 and keeps the CNN-over-ViT ordering (Table~\ref{tab:cifar100_results}). Across the same four protocols the measured CNN$\rightarrow$ViT asymmetry runs from $+4.96\pm1.01$ points (target-correct) to $+18.53\pm0.71$ points (unconditioned), with $+10.92\pm0.34$ under both-correct and $+17.50\pm0.92$ under successful-source. The mean per-seed spread is $13.83\pm1.30$ points, against $15.01\pm0.84$ on CIFAR-10, so the harder dataset leaves the protocol effect in the same range, slightly below the CIFAR-10 value. The direction is positive in all twelve measurements (four protocols $\times$ three seed pairs), as it was on CIFAR-10. Under the both-correct protocol the paired difference is $10.92$ points. As on CIFAR-10, the quoted interval $[9.47, 12.35]$ is the average of the three per-seed bootstrap endpoints and has no coverage guarantee; the across-run $t$ interval, which is the one that matches a claim about architectures, is $[10.07, 11.77]$. The sign-flip permutation $p$ is below the resolution of $20{,}000$ permutations in every seed, and TOST does not establish equivalence at any margin tested.

One of the three registered predictions is confirmed, one fails under the reading we treat as binding, and the third is not testable in this design. The prediction that the sign would be preserved is confirmed ($12/12$). The prediction that the protocol spread would be larger on the harder dataset was registered as a comparison against the CIFAR-10 spread, quoted at the time as $10.45$ points. Against that literal threshold it holds ($13.83 > 10.45$); against the CIFAR-10 spread as it now stands under the strict successful-source reading it does not ($13.83 < 15.01$). We count this as a failure, since the registration named the CIFAR-10 spread and not the number. The third predicted that the deviation of the unconditioned from the conditioned rate would grow with the target's clean error. The measured slope is $+0.656$ points per point of target error, positive as predicted; but on CIFAR-100 two of the three targets have clean errors that differ by $0.21$ points ($36.15$ and $36.36$), so the correlation is computed over two effective clusters, not three, and its value ($r = 0.931$ over six directions, $0.974$ aggregated to targets) rests on a two-point contrast. The measurement is \emph{consistent with} the prediction without \emph{testing} it, and it does not count as a third confirmation.

One registered endpoint was missed: we predicted clean accuracy in the $33$ to $40\%$ band for the adversarially trained ViT and measured $43.17\pm1.10\%$. The band assumed that the clean-accuracy cost of adversarial training scales the same way for both architectures. It does not: the ViT retained $0.885$ of its clean pre-training accuracy under adversarial training, against $0.802$ for the ResNet. The two adversarial endpoints, which are the ones the protocol claim depends on, fell inside their bands (PGD-10 predicted $10$ to $14\%$, measured $11.15$; AutoAttack predicted $8$ to $11\%$, measured $8.87$). Conditioning also changes the class mix, so an asymmetry could in principle be a composition artifact; it is not. Under target-correct the composition component of the asymmetry is $-1.249$, $-1.330$ and $-1.065$ points across the three seed pairs, \emph{negative}, so rebalancing the class mix would make the asymmetry larger; under successful-source it is small and positive in every seed ($+0.527$, $+0.435$ and $+0.382$ points), and under the unconditioned and both-correct protocols it is exactly zero, as the decomposition requires.

This result carries two qualifications. The two adversarial bands held with margins of $0.22$ points (AutoAttack) and $0.48$ points (PGD-10) at the nearest seed-level edge, smaller than the checkpoint-selection noise on this same dataset: sweeping early-stopping patience and curve smoothing over the CIFAR-100 trajectories, with the training run held fixed, moves the reported PGD-10 accuracy by $0.18$ to $1.83$ points (mean $0.86$). The same nine-cell sweep on CIFAR-10 gives $0.54$ to $2.83$ points per validation split, and the eighteen-cell figure of Section~\ref{subsec:selection} is larger still because it also varies the split, a dimension the CIFAR-100 protocol does not have. Second, the same held-out split serves selection at both stages instead of two independent splits, a choice inherited from the CIFAR-10 protocol to keep the two datasets comparable.

\subsubsection{A third dataset with a near-matched pair: SVHN}
\label{subsec:svhn}

CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 both place the two architectures far apart in clean accuracy (about $12$ and $21$ points). SVHN~\cite{netzer2011reading} does not. Adversarially trained under the same recipe, the ResNet reaches $94.76$ and $93.77\%$ clean accuracy in the two runs (mean $94.27$) and the ViT $92.82$ and $92.03\%$ (mean $92.42$), gaps of $1.94$ and $1.74$ points (mean $1.84$); the two-run standard deviations, $0.70$ and $0.56$, carry little information, so we give the run values. Mean robust accuracies are $55.52$ and $52.30\%$ under PGD-10. We call this pair near-matched, not matched, since the PGD-10 gap is still about three points. If the identity term follows the clean-accuracy gap, the direction itself should become a matter of protocol here.

It does. Two of the four protocols report a CNN advantage and two a ViT advantage (Table~\ref{tab:protocol_settings}). The two protocols we endorse for directional claims, target-correct and both-correct, agree with each other and both favor the ViT; the sign change separates them from the unconditioned and successful-source rates. The unconditioned reversal follows arithmetically from Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_identity}: the ViT target's clean error exceeds the CNN's by about two points here, which is enough to carry a $-1.00$ target-correct difference to $+0.39$ raw. Under both-correct, which we treat as primary because it scores both directions on identical samples, the difference is $-0.35$ points, with a sign-flip permutation $p$ of $0.105$ and TOST equivalence accepted at all three margins, including the tightest one we test ($\pm1$ point). The protocol decides whether such an equivalence claim is available at all: at the $\pm2$ margin fixed before measurement no successful-source estimate could pass, since both point estimates ($+2.98$ and $+2.41$) lie outside the margin, while both both-correct estimates ($-0.34$ and $-0.36$) lie well inside it. The acceptance needs one qualification. SVHN was run with two seed pairs, so the quoted interval $[-0.76, +0.07]$ averages the endpoints of two per-seed bootstraps and no across-run interval is available; the equivalence rests on the within-run comparison and on the close agreement of the two runs, and two runs cannot bound the run-to-run variation. A study reporting only the successful-source rate would announce a $2.70$-point CNN advantage on SVHN; one reporting only the target-correct rate would announce a $1.00$-point ViT advantage.

The four-protocol spread on SVHN is $4.14$ and $3.26$ points in the two pairs (mean $3.70$), against $15.01\pm0.84$ on CIFAR-10 and $13.83\pm1.30$ on CIFAR-100, while the clean-accuracy gaps are $1.84$, $\approx12$ and $\approx21$ points; the three-protocol spread, which is the identity term, is $1.49$ and $1.30$ points. The relation is not monotone across the three datasets: the largest gap does not produce the largest spread. What separates SVHN is the near-matched pair. Once the spread is small enough it no longer clears the asymmetry itself, so the sign becomes a property of the protocol and not of the architectures, and the direction reported as stable in Section~\ref{subsec:transfer} is stable \emph{in the regime where the two models differ in clean accuracy}, which both CIFAR datasets occupy. SVHN was run in a reduced configuration fixed in advance: two seed pairs instead of three, $50$ adversarial-training epochs, no AutoAttack, and no third architecture, so the $3\times3$ control of Section~\ref{subsubsec:matrix} is not built here.

\subsubsection{Spread and the clean-accuracy gap}
\label{subsubsec:spread_gap}

% REF: Uc-protokol egimi, SVHN dahil (+0.4697 [+0.3606; +0.6649]; dort protokol -0.1331 [-0.5700; +0.4686];
%      yalniz ozdeslik terimi +0.4712 [+0.3615; +0.6666]; 19/20 esitlik; en buyuk sapma 0.16; 20 nokta, 8 kume)
The settings above suggest that the spread grows with the clean-accuracy gap, and the analysis registered before the CIFAR-100 and SVHN measurements tested this on the direction pairs of all final models ($20$ direction pairs from eight seed-and-dataset clusters, cluster bootstrap). The four-protocol slope, $-0.133$ with interval $[-0.570, +0.469]$, includes zero, and we withdraw it. The three-protocol slope, which leaves out successful-source, is $+0.470$ $[+0.361, +0.665]$. That slope is not independent evidence for a mechanism. In $19$ of the $20$ direction pairs the three-protocol spread equals the absolute difference between the unconditioned and target-correct asymmetries, because the both-correct estimate lies between them, and that difference agrees with the prediction of Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_identity} to within $0.16$ points; the identity term alone gives a slope of $+0.471$ $[+0.3615, +0.6666]$. The three-protocol slope is the identity measured a second time, and we read it as an out-of-sample check of Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_identity}.

The pool first used for this fit excluded the SVHN pair. That departs from the registration, which placed SVHN in the fitted arm and named it as the pair that would fill the empty band of small gaps; the reassignment to an independent consistency check was written after the SVHN result was in hand, so the SVHN-inclusive fit above is the binding one. On the SVHN-excluded pool the four-protocol slope is $-0.528$ $[-0.664, -0.418]$ and the three-protocol slope $+0.431$ $[+0.333, +0.633]$. The opposite signs come from the successful-source protocol, which is the extreme of the four in $16$ of the $18$ pairs there, with target-correct against successful-source the widest pair ($23.77$ points average gap, widest in $13$ of the $18$ directions). A controlled arm calibrates the same relation inside training trajectories: one member of a pair is held fixed and the other is swept along its own trajectory, so architecture, seed, dataset and budget stay constant while the gap moves ($116$ points from eight clusters, cluster bootstrap at the trajectory level). Its three-protocol slope, $+0.673$ $[+0.602, +0.727]$, has the sign of the observational one and carries the same caveat, since it too is dominated by the identity term. Its four-protocol slope, $+0.273$ $[+0.219, +0.371]$, lies entirely inside the observational interval on the binding pool, so on that quantity the arms neither agree nor disagree, and restricting the observational pool to the matched architecture pair moves the four-protocol slope to $-0.100$ $[-0.464, +0.092]$. A quantity this sensitive to which pairs enter the fit is not one a mechanism claim can rest on. With six to eight clusters a percentile cluster bootstrap undercovers, so all of these intervals understate their width, and the WideResNet, a single fixed checkpoint, appears in every observational cluster.

The four-protocol spread does not follow the gap (slope $+0.092$, $r = 0.053$), and among robust pairs of nearly equal clean accuracy it stays large: $9.03$ points for the two WideResNet-28-10 checkpoints of Carmon et al.~\cite{carmon2019unlabeled} and Gowal et al.~\cite{gowal2020uncovering} ($0.21$ points apart), $12.73$ for the XCiT-S12 against the Gowal et al.\ model ($0.58$) and $17.50$ for our ResNet-18 against the Engstrom et al.~\cite{engstrom2019robustness} ResNet-50 ($1.66$). Panel (b) shows why: the successful-source term is unrelated to the gap, close to zero for the standard-trained pairs, whose PGD-10 robust accuracy is near zero so that almost every source attack succeeds, and large and of either sign among robust models. These slopes describe the pool and test nothing, because the pairs share models and runs.

% REF: Yayilim - temiz fark iliskisi, grup araliklari (SVHN uc protokol 1.30; 1.49)
% REF: Standart egitimli hedeflerde oncul p, cift turune gore ozet (0.47+-0.16; 0.19+-0.02)
% REF: RobustBench havuzu (AT hedeflerde p en kucuk 0.9846); Turetilmis (SVHN temiz fark ort. 1.84)
The matched-pair prediction is a consequence of the identity, not a separate finding. The unconditioned, target-correct and both-correct estimates differ through terms that scale with the clean errors, so a pair matched in clean accuracy should leave them close together whatever its architectures. What the data contribute is the three measured facts the prediction needs: $p$ is at least $0.984$ on every adversarially trained target we examined, the both-correct estimate lies between the other two at $57$ of the $60$ points, and the successful-source term does not depend on the gap. Accordingly SVHN, at a $1.84$-point gap, has a three-protocol spread of $1.49$ and $1.30$ points in its two pairs, and the standard-trained ResNet-18 and WideResNet-28-10, $0.47\pm0.16$ points apart, have all four protocols within $0.19\pm0.02$ points (Section~\ref{subsec:external}). Of the $15$-point CIFAR-10 range, the parts that come from the clean-accuracy difference, the identity term and the distance within the endorsed pair, should shrink for pairs matched in clean accuracy; the successful-source excess need not, as the robust pairs above show.

\subsection{Standard-Trained Targets and an External Model Pool}
\label{subsec:external}

% REF: Standart egitimli hedeflerde oncul p (temiz ciftler); RobustBench havuzu (8 model, tohum 42)
\begin{sloppypar}
Two questions remain that our own adversarially trained pair cannot answer: whether the premise $p$ holds on standard-trained targets, the regime most transfer studies attack, and whether the matched-pair prediction holds for models trained by others. Both are answered without new training. First, the clean-trained ResNet-18 and ViT-Tiny checkpoints of the three training pairs, the starting points of adversarial fine-tuning, are combined with the standard-trained WideResNet-28-10 distributed by RobustBench~\cite{croce2021robustbench} (identifier \texttt{Standard}) into one $3\times3$ matrix per pair. Second, an eight-model CIFAR-10 pool joins our adversarially trained ResNet-18 and ViT-Tiny of the first pair, the standard-trained WideResNet and five robust RobustBench checkpoints, listed here by their RobustBench identifiers: a PreAct ResNet-18 (\texttt{Wong2020Fast})~\cite{wong2020fast}, a ResNet-50 (\texttt{Engstrom2019Robustness})~\cite{engstrom2019robustness}, a WideResNet-28-10 trained with unlabeled data (\texttt{Carmon2019Unlabeled})~\cite{carmon2019unlabeled}, an XCiT-S12, which combines attention with convolutional components (\texttt{Debenedetti2022Light\_XCiT-S12})~\cite{debenedetti2023light}, and the WideResNet-28-10 reference of the previous sections~\cite{gowal2020uncovering}. Every direction is attacked with PGD-10 at $\eps=8/255$ on the full test set with per-sample logging, and all four protocols, $p$ and the $p=1$ residual are computed from those records (Table~\ref{tab:external_pool}).
\end{sloppypar}

On standard-trained targets the premise weakens. There Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_identity} is an upper bound, and the general form, Equation~\eqref{eq:general_identity}, with measured $p$, is the correction to use. In the pool, where seven of the eight targets are adversarially trained, $p\geq0.984$ on every adversarially trained target and $p\geq0.933$ on the standard-trained one, and the $p=1$ residual stays within $0.35$ points over all $56$ directions; at the last checkpoint of our own pairs it stays within $0.20$ (Section~\ref{subsec:selection}).

\subsection{Checkpoint Selection and the Asymmetry}
\label{subsec:selection}

The offline rule that picks the final checkpoint from a fixed trajectory is usually left implicit, although robust overfitting makes that choice consequential in adversarial training~\cite{rice2020overfitting}. Holding a training trajectory fixed and varying only how its final checkpoint is chosen (validation split, early-stopping patience, and whether the validation curve is smoothed before the argmax; eighteen combinations) moves the reported PGD-10 accuracy by $2.62$ to $2.85$ points per seed for the ResNet and $1.58$ to $2.09$ points for the ViT. The weights on disk are identical across these cells; only the rule that picks one of them differs. About half of that dispersion comes from one of the three dimensions. Smoothing the validation curve before the argmax lowers test PGD-10 accuracy monotonically in every ResNet run and in two of the three ViT runs, so it is a systematically worse rule; restricted to the split and patience choices that studies actually vary, the dispersion falls to $1.09$ times the seed-level standard deviation for the ResNet and $1.42$ times for the ViT. The dispersion is quoted in absolute units and deliberately not as a ratio to seed-level dispersion, because that ratio depends on which of the eighteen cells is the reference: across all eighteen it runs from $0.67$ to $3.38$ for the ResNet and $0.63$ to $7.38$ for the ViT, inverting the comparison for some of them.

The CIFAR-100 runs supply a counterweight: the epoch selected by our own protocol varies by $25$ epochs across the three ResNet seeds, yet the resulting test PGD-10 standard deviation is $0.32$ points. The selection \emph{path} was unstable while the selected \emph{outcome} was not; which of the two happens depends on the plateau of the trajectory, not on the architecture.

The selection rule also reaches the transfer asymmetry, because it sets the clean-accuracy gap on which the spread depends. Re-scoring the three training pairs at their last checkpoint, the epoch at which early stopping ended training (epochs $71$, $76$ and $77$ for the ResNets and $45$, $38$ and $39$ for the ViTs, twenty epochs after the selected $51$, $56$, $57$ and $25$, $18$, $19$), instead of the selected one narrows the clean gap from $12.26\pm0.90$ to $10.55\pm0.59$ points (change $-1.71\pm0.84$) and the four-protocol spread from $15.01\pm0.84$ to $12.84\pm0.34$ points (change $-2.16\pm0.93$); the three-protocol spread falls from $9.21\pm0.76$ to $7.84\pm0.56$. The target-correct asymmetry barely moves ($+4.64\pm0.56$ against $+4.36\pm0.44$), the successful-source estimate falls from $+19.37$ to $+17.48$, all four protocols keep the CNN$\rightarrow$ViT sign in every pair, and the premise holds as before ($p\geq0.9915$, $p=1$ residual at most $0.20$ points).

The stored training trajectories give the same picture across training. They come from CIFAR-10 retraining runs with the same seeds, not from the selected checkpoints of the main pairs, and from the CIFAR-100 runs themselves, saved about every ten epochs. On CIFAR-10 these retraining runs keep the clean gap at or above $10.59$ points at both the selected and the final epoch, so selection moves the gap by about $1.7$ points but does not remove it. Its exact size is selection-sensitive, though. Moving one member of a pair from its selected to its final epoch changes the four-protocol spread by $-1.53\pm1.15$ points when the ResNet is moved and by $-0.03\pm1.15$ when the ViT is, and moving the ViT shifts the target-correct asymmetry by $+2.53\pm0.15$ points; on CIFAR-100 the spread changes by $-1.84\pm0.94$ and $+0.84\pm0.24$ points. The selection rule thus moves the level of the asymmetry by up to about two and a half points and its spread by about two, without changing the direction or the order of magnitude. It has to be stated in the methods section.

\subsection{Three Readings That Do Not Survive Direct Measurement}
\label{subsec:readings}

Architecture comparisons invite three readings beyond the transfer asymmetry: that CNN gradients are spatially more localized, that adversarial perturbation degrades ViT attention, and that ViT-specific transfer attacks improve ViT-sourced transfer. Each was measured directly, none holds in this regime, and the same measurements leave two behavioral differences standing.

Where adversarial damage accumulates along depth differs between the architectures (Table~\ref{tab:feature_degradation}; residual block outputs, $n=1000$ per run), extending layer-wise analyses of clean-trained ViTs~\cite{raghu2021vision} to the adversarially trained setting. In the ViT the drift is gradual and direction-only: similarity declines to a minimum at block 11 and recovers slightly at the output, while feature norms stay within $0.45\%$ of their clean values. In the CNN the damage is concentrated: the first block of the last stage falls to $0.8777\pm0.0181$ with a $-13.14\pm1.89\%$ norm contraction, and the final block partly repairs the representation ($0.9108\pm0.0060$). At their minima the CLS pathway drifts about three times as far as the patch-token mean ($0.9604$ versus $0.9870$, a ratio of $3.05$ in $1-\cos$), at different depths (blocks 11 and 9), and read at the MLP sub-block output instead of the residual block output the minima move to $0.9343$ at block 10 and $0.9840$ at block 8. The attention reading does not hold. Adversarial perturbation leaves the entropy of the CLS attention distribution unchanged: the largest per-layer mean change is $0.0045$ nats, against clean layer means between $4.61$ and $5.11$ nats, and with a margin of $\pm0.05$ nats fixed from the scale of the measure, run-level TOST over the three runs accepts equivalence in all $12$ layers, the weakest at $p=0.006$ (Figure~\ref{fig:feature_degradation}a). What grows with depth is the displacement of the attention map, from $0.028$ total variation in the first layer to $0.084$ in the last (panel b), so the attack redirects attention slightly without broadening or sharpening it; the single-sample maps of Figure~\ref{fig:attention_layers} show redirection, not diffusion, and an earlier 8-sample figure batch had suggested an attention effect that this measurement does not support. Feature drift does not separate flipped from unflipped samples either; the unflipped group here also contains the samples a model already misclassifies on clean input. The last-block cosine is $0.9895$ for flipped against $0.9877$ for unflipped ViT samples, and $0.9058$ against $0.9143$ for the CNN; neither difference is significant at the run level (paired $t$ over three runs, $p=0.20$ and $p=0.080$), although the CNN difference has the same sign in all three runs. Attention-shift effects under patch perturbations~\cite{gu2022vision} and attacks on the attention mechanism itself~\cite{fu2022patchfool} belong to a different threat model.

A natural objection to the transfer results is that gradient attacks treating the ViT as a generic differentiable model handicap the ViT-sourced direction, since attacks designed for transformer structure are reported to transfer better. Token Gradient Regularization (TGR)~\cite{zhang2023tgr}, which suppresses extreme-valued token gradients in the attention, QKV and MLP paths of the source during backpropagation, tests the objection; our adaptation applies it to the CIFAR-10 ViT-Tiny at the budget used throughout (Section~\ref{subsec:attack_methods}), with plain MI-FGSM generated in the same run as a paired control. TGR does not improve transfer in this regime; it degrades it (Table~\ref{tab:tgr}). It gives up 14.9 points of white-box strength on the source, and conditioned transfer falls by 3.0 points under target-correct and by 2.2 under both-correct, with paired McNemar tests on the both-correct subset favoring MI-FGSM in every run ($p = 6.1\times10^{-10}$, $1.1\times10^{-27}$, $7.0\times10^{-34}$). The targets are adversarially trained, the inputs are $32\times32$ CIFAR-10 images upsampled inside the model and the source is a 5.7M-parameter ViT-Tiny, whereas TGR was built for standard-trained ImageNet ViTs, so the result bounds our adaptation in this regime and not the method in general. For this paper its consequence is that the CNN$\rightarrow$ViT asymmetry is not an artifact of architecture-agnostic attacks: giving the ViT direction a transformer-specific attack makes it weaker.

\subsection{Sources of Variance}
\label{subsec:statistics_results}

Four sources of variance bear on the comparisons above. Attack-initialization stochasticity is negligible: re-running PGD-10 with three attack seeds (42, 123, 456) on a fixed 1{,}000-sample subset leaves clean accuracy exactly unchanged, since the loader does not shuffle, and moves robust accuracy by a standard deviation of $0.05$ points for the CNN and $0.00$ for the ViT. Training-run variance is larger but still small: across the three independent runs per architecture, the standard deviation for the adversarially trained pair is at most $0.55$ points on clean accuracy and $0.50$ on PGD-10 (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}).

% REF: Secim kuralinin asimetriye etkisi, B (kosumlar arasi aralik en cok 2.41)
Comparing the protocol effect against those two figures directly would be an error of scale, because the protocol effect is measured on the transfer asymmetry while both figures above are measured on absolute accuracy. We therefore quantify the protocol and training-run effects on the \emph{same} quantity, the off-diagonal asymmetry, and report both in absolute units. Within a fixed seed pair, changing only the conditioning protocol moves the asymmetry by $15.01\pm0.84$ points. This figure is the mean of the per-seed ranges; it coincides with the range of the protocol means here because the extremal protocols are the same in all three seeds, and differs on CIFAR-100 ($13.83$ against $13.58$) because there they are not. Retraining both architectures moves that same asymmetry across a range of at most $2.41$ points, with a standard deviation of $0.23$ to $1.27$ points depending on the protocol it is measured under (Section~\ref{subsec:transfer}). Fifteen points against at most two and a half is the ordering the paper rests on. Changing the checkpoint-selection rule moves the four-protocol spread by $2.16$ points on average (Section~\ref{subsec:selection}), about as much as retraining moves the asymmetry within a protocol ($2.41$ at most), so it leaves that ordering intact.

We do not headline the ratio of these two spreads. With three runs the denominator carries two degrees of freedom, so a $\chi^2$ interval on any single ratio is very wide: for the successful-source protocol it runs from $0.8$ to $9.9$ and includes unity. Reported as a sensitivity, the ratio spans $5.2$ to $32.6$ across dispersion measures and reference protocols, and equals $28.3$ under the like-for-like comparison on the both-correct protocol we treat as primary. Two cautions bound it further: the protocol with the largest asymmetry also carries the largest run-to-run standard deviation, so numerator and denominator are not independent, and because all three runs share one validation split the denominator excludes split-to-split variance, which makes the ratio an upper bound. The absolute comparison survives both, which is why it and not the fold-change carries the claim. The fourth source, the checkpoint-selection rule, is treated in Section~\ref{subsec:selection}.

The three CIFAR-10 training pairs and their CIFAR-10 evaluations, like the earlier checkpoint pair mentioned in Section~\ref{subsec:training}, were run on an NVIDIA RTX 5060 Ti (16GB VRAM); the CIFAR-100, SVHN and $L_2$ campaigns, the checkpoint-selection trajectories and the standard-trained, last-checkpoint and RobustBench-pool transfer runs were run on an RTX 5090 (32GB). All runs used PyTorch 2.6.0 with CUDA 12.8 and deterministic cuDNN settings. AutoAttack evaluation used the standard configuration (APGD-CE, APGD-T, FAB-T, Square) on the full 10{,}000-sample test set in seeded 1{,}000-sample chunks with per-sample outcome logging. The three-seed training pipeline required 21.7 GPU-hours in total.
