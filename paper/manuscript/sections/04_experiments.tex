The results indicate a statistically significant \emph{absolute} robustness advantage for the CNN under standard adversarial training: on the full test set, ResNet-18 AT achieves 35.74\% robust accuracy under AutoAttack, compared to 32.94\% for ViT-Tiny AT---a 2.80 percentage-point gap. Because both models are evaluated on the same 10{,}000 samples with per-sample outcomes logged, we can apply McNemar's paired test: 940 samples are robustly classified only by the CNN versus 660 only by the ViT ($\chi^2 = 48.65$, $p < 10^{-11}$), so the gap is not attributable to evaluation noise. The 95\% binomial confidence half-widths are below $\pm$1 percentage point per model. Two caveats remain. First, the gap is modest relative to training-level variability: across our training runs the ViT's PGD-10 accuracy varied by roughly four points (Section~\ref{subsec:statistics_results}), so the architecture comparison should be read as ``under a matched adversarial objective and threat model'' rather than as a universal constant. Second, the CNN also retains a higher clean accuracy (85.42\% vs 75.65\%, a 9.8-point gap), reflecting the difficulty of training ViTs on small datasets from scratch. AutoAttack robustness is consistently lower than PGD-10 robustness for both architectures (5.23 points for ResNet-18, 3.15 points for ViT-Tiny), confirming the importance of using this stronger benchmark. The WideResNet-28-10 baseline~\cite{gowal2020uncovering}, at 62.76\% robust accuracy, demonstrates how far capacity, additional training data, and a stronger training recipe jointly push robustness beyond architectural choice alone.

Table~\ref{tab:conditional} decomposes these absolute scores into their clean-accuracy and conditional-susceptibility components (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}). The decomposition changes the reading of the headline result in three ways. Under PGD-10, the conditioned fooling rates of the two models are statistically indistinguishable (52.15\% vs 52.33\%, overlapping CIs), so the entire absolute PGD gap is accounted for by the clean-accuracy gap: $85.42 \times 0.478$ vs $75.65 \times 0.477$. Under AutoAttack, the per-model conditional ordering even reverses slightly in the ViT's favor (58.16\% vs 56.46\% fooling, CIs nearly touching): on the samples it solves cleanly, the ViT loses proportionally \emph{less} to the stronger attack ensemble. The jointly solved subset tells a third story: restricted to the 7{,}260 samples both models classify correctly---an exactly paired comparison on shared inputs---the CNN retains a significant advantage under both attacks (PGD-10: 54.92\% vs 49.61\%, McNemar $\chi^2 = 88.1$, $p < 10^{-20}$; AutoAttack: 48.15\% vs 45.33\%, $\chi^2 = 27.4$, $p < 10^{-6}$). These views reconcile compositionally: the samples that only the CNN solves cleanly ($n$=1{,}282) are almost never robust (below 8\% PGD survival), which dilutes the CNN's per-model conditional rate down to the ViT's level. The headline ``the CNN is more robust'' therefore holds absolutely and on the common subset, while per-model conditional susceptibility is essentially matched under PGD-10 and slightly ViT-favorable under AutoAttack---a multi-view reading we carry through the rest of the paper.

\label{subsec:transfer}

The multi-protocol analysis (Table~\ref{tab:transfer_protocols}) yields a sharper methodological finding than either single metric would. The raw rates show an apparent 8.3-point asymmetry favoring CNN sources (40.2\% vs 31.9\%): because the ViT target has roughly ten points more clean error than the CNN target, the raw metric credits the CNN source with failures the ViT would have made on clean inputs anyway. Under the target-correct conditioned metric this asymmetry vanishes: 20.95\% vs 20.32\%, a 0.63-point difference that is not significant ($z=0.98$, $p=0.33$) and is formally equivalent within a $\pm$2-point margin (TOST, $p=0.016$; equivalence also holds at $\pm$3 but not at $\pm$1, $p=0.72$)---and this target-correct symmetry is stable under the momentum-based MI-FGSM~\cite{dong2018boosting} (19.85\% [18.97, 20.78] vs 20.11\% [19.28, 20.96]). However, the two protocols that restrict the comparison further \emph{reverse the conclusion again}: on the both-correct subset the directions separate decisively (19.19\% vs 13.86\%, a 5.33-point CNN$\rightarrow$ViT advantage; paired bootstrap CI [4.39, 6.25], sign-flip permutation $p < 5 \times 10^{-5}$, TOST equivalence rejected even at $\pm$3 points), and the successful-source protocol agrees (5.28-point gap). The mechanism is compositional: samples that the target solves cleanly but the \emph{source} does not are hyper-vulnerable in both directions (56.9\% fooling for ViT$\rightarrow$CNN on its $n$=1{,}282 such samples; 63.0\% for CNN$\rightarrow$ViT on its $n$=305), and the asymmetric sizes of these marginal subsets happen to pull the two target-correct rates to parity. Applying the identical target-correct protocol to the \emph{clean-trained} counterparts underlines the same lesson from another angle: conditioned transfer is much lower (9.91\% [9.32, 10.53] CNN$\rightarrow$ViT vs 11.76\% [11.15, 12.41] ViT$\rightarrow$CNN, a small but distinguishable asymmetry), while the raw metric would report a spurious 12.2-point gap in the \emph{opposite} direction (28.3\% vs 16.1\%). Taken together, the cautionary result is stronger than a clean-error confound alone: \emph{the measured direction and size of cross-architecture transfer asymmetry depend on the conditioning protocol}, which can create (raw), erase (target-correct), or reveal (both-correct, successful-source) a directional difference on the same underlying data. Cross-architecture transfer studies should therefore state their protocol explicitly and, where feasible, report more than one; none of the protocol readings here licenses interpreting raw-rate asymmetries architecturally.

\subsection{Gradient Characteristics}
\label{subsec:gradient}

To characterize architectural differences in input sensitivity, we analyze the gradient properties of both architectures, together with a CIFAR-native ViT control (32$\times$32 inputs, 4$\times$4 patches, no upsampling) trained with the same adversarial protocol, which isolates the effect of the 32$\rightarrow$224 upsampling in our main ViT pipeline. Table~\ref{tab:gradient} summarizes the key statistics using scale-invariant sparsity measures (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}); gradients are computed with per-sample losses so that norms do not depend on the evaluation batch size.

All ResNet--ViT differences on the scale-invariant sparsity metrics are significant under per-sample paired Wilcoxon tests (Holm-corrected $p < 10^{-15}$; paired Cohen's $d$ = 0.34--0.65).}

The gradient analysis reveals consistent but modest architectural differences. Across all three scale-invariant sparsity measures, CNN gradients are sparser than ViT gradients (Hoyer 0.474 vs 0.449; Gini 0.634 vs 0.606; 31.5\% vs 25.8\% of components below 1\% of the per-sample maximum), indicating that CNN sensitivity is somewhat more concentrated in fewer input components. Because the same 500 samples are measured under both models, we test these differences with per-sample paired statistics: all three are highly significant (Wilcoxon signed-rank, Holm-corrected $p < 10^{-15}$) with small-to-medium paired effect sizes (Cohen's $d$ of 0.34, 0.65, and 0.43 for Hoyer, Gini, and the relative-threshold fraction), quantifying ``consistent but modest'' precisely. Interestingly, this ordering is a product of adversarial training: in the clean-trained counterparts the ViT is the sparser model (Hoyer 0.406 vs 0.354), and adversarial training sparsifies the CNN far more strongly ($+$0.12 Hoyer) than the ViT ($+$0.04), consistent with the known sparsifying effect of adversarial training on input gradients~\cite{chalasani2020concise}. The native-resolution ViT control is \emph{less} sparse than the upsampled ViT on every measure, so the CNN--ViT sparsity ordering is not an artifact of the 32$\rightarrow$224 upsampling---if anything, upsampling moves the ViT \emph{toward} the CNN. We emphasize that these differences are far smaller than a threshold-based sparsity metric would suggest: an earlier version of this analysis (fixed $10^{-6}$ threshold, measured on the preliminary run2 models) indicated a 1.7$\times$ sparsity ratio, most of which reflected gradient-scale differences rather than structure; on the final models the same scale-dependent metric inflates the ratio even further ($\approx$2.4$\times$). Cross-sample gradient alignment is low for both architectures but measurably higher for the ViT (0.052 vs 0.038 in mean absolute cosine over all pairs, a 1.36$\times$ ratio; native control 0.079), suggesting that transformer sensitivity axes are somewhat more shared across inputs. The \emph{signed} mean cosine, by contrast, is near zero for every model ($\leq$0.002): what is shared across samples is sensitivity axes, not consistent directions, so the alignment gap should not be read as direct evidence of cheap universal perturbations. We treat both characteristics as behavioral correlates of, rather than established explanations for, the architectures' differing adversarial behavior.

\subsection{Feature Degradation in Vision Transformers}
\label{subsec:attention}

We analyze how adversarial perturbations affect ViT feature representations across layers, extending layer-wise representation analyses of clean-trained ViTs~\cite{raghu2021vision} to the adversarially trained setting. The measured representations are the outputs of each transformer block's MLP sub-block; the attention-weight analysis accompanies the figure-level results. Table~\ref{tab:feature_degradation} shows all twelve blocks ($n$=100 test samples).

The layer-wise analysis reveals a characteristic degradation profile. Cosine similarity between clean and adversarial features drops quickly in the early blocks (0.994 at Block~0 to 0.972 by Block~4), reaches its minimum in the middle-to-late network (0.955 at Block~8), and then plateaus with a slight recovery in the final blocks (0.965 at Blocks~10--11). Most of the directional deviation is therefore accumulated by mid-network rather than growing monotonically to the output---an ``early-drop, mid-network-plateau'' profile rather than strictly progressive degradation. The final-block recovery is statistically reliable at the batch level (Block~8 vs Block~11 cosine similarity, Welch $t=-4.07$, $p=0.005$). A parallel analysis of the adversarially trained ResNet-18's eight residual-block outputs (same attack, same metrics, $n$=100) shows a qualitatively different profile: cosine similarity degrades \emph{monotonically} with depth (0.995 at layer1.0 down to 0.913 at layer4.1, with no plateau or recovery), accompanied by much larger norm distortions in the final blocks ($-$6.4\% at layer4.0, $+$7.2\% at layer4.1). The plateau-and-recovery shape with preserved norms is therefore specific to the evaluated ViT in our model pair, not a generic property of deep networks under attack. Feature norm changes remain modest throughout (at most $-$1.4\%), indicating that adversarial perturbations corrupt the \emph{direction} of representations far more than their magnitude. One untested hypothesis is that the mild recovery in the final blocks reflects a regularizing influence of the classifier head on the last transformer blocks. Even so, the terminal deviation (cosine similarity $\approx$0.965) is sufficient to flip predictions on most attacked samples: on these 100 samples, accuracy drops from 79\% clean to 39\% under PGD-10.

Figure~\ref{fig:tsne_features} shows an illustrative t-SNE embedding of the penultimate feature spaces; because t-SNE embeddings are sensitive to hyperparameters, we base all claims on quantitative metrics computed in the original feature space ($n$=500, white-box PGD-10 per model). These metrics show that the attack collapses class structure most strongly in the \emph{CNN}: clean-fit 5-NN label consistency drops from 85.2\% to 47.8\%, the silhouette score falls from 0.14 to $-$0.06, the intra/inter-class distance ratio rises from 0.64 to 0.91, and the relative class-centroid shift is 0.29. The ViT's penultimate space, by contrast, is only weakly class-separated even on clean inputs (silhouette $\approx$0; 5-NN consistency 68.2\%, dropping to 53.4\% under attack) and its centroids shift far less (0.09). An earlier qualitative reading of these plots (``more class-coherent CNN displacement, greater ViT inter-class confusion'') is not supported by the quantitative metrics and we do not maintain it; the measurable statement is that the CNN's cleanly well-separated feature space is heavily disrupted by white-box attack, whereas the ViT's feature-space class structure is weaker to begin with and degrades less in these aggregate terms.

\subsection{Statistical Validation}
\label{subsec:statistics_results}

To quantify evaluation stochasticity, we evaluate each trained model across 3 different random seeds (42, 123, 456) for attack initialization on 1,000 test samples. Table~\ref{tab:statistical} shows the results.

\begin{table}[!t]
\centering

The low variance confirms that evaluation stochasticity is negligible. The evaluated subset and its ordering are identical across seeds (the test loader does not shuffle), so clean accuracy shows exactly zero variance and the only stochastic element is the random initialization of PGD restarts. Robust accuracy shows minimal variation (standard deviation 0.12 percentage points for CNN, 0.09 for ViT), indicating stable attack behavior across random restarts. This procedure quantifies attack-initialization stochasticity only; it does not measure data-sampling or training-level variance. (Differences between Table~\ref{tab:statistical} and Table~\ref{tab:main_results} PGD values---0.4 points for the CNN and up to 2.1 points for the ViT---reflect the easier first-1{,}000-sample subset versus the full test set.)

Beyond evaluation seeds, we conducted multiple independent adversarial-training runs per architecture during this study. Under the final training protocol, ResNet-18 AT reached 40.97\% and ViT-Tiny AT 36.09\% full-test PGD-10 accuracy; earlier runs under a preliminary implementation of the training loop (differing in attack-generation details, later standardized as described in Section~\ref{subsec:defense_methods}) produced values in the 40.3--41.0\% (CNN) and 32.8--36.9\% (ViT) ranges. The CNN-over-ViT ranking was preserved in every run, though the ViT's run-to-run variation is clearly larger than evaluation-level variance, and we therefore avoid attributing precise magnitudes to the architecture gap beyond the paired analysis of Section~\ref{subsec:robustness}.

\textbf{Computational Details:} All experiments were conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 5060 Ti (16GB VRAM) using PyTorch 2.6.0 with CUDA 12.8. AutoAttack evaluation used the standard configuration (APGD-CE, APGD-T, FAB-T, Square) on the full 10{,}000-sample test set, evaluated in seeded 1{,}000-sample chunks with per-sample outcome logging; total wall-clock time was several hours per model on this hardware.
