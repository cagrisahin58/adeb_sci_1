Table~\ref{tab:conditional} decomposes the absolute scores into their clean-accuracy and conditional-susceptibility components (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}). Because misclassified clean inputs count as non-robust, the factorization is exact: for AutoAttack it reads $85.78 \times 0.442 = 37.93$ for the CNN and $73.53 \times 0.396 = 29.14$ for the ViT. Both factors favor the CNN. It starts from a 12.3-point higher clean accuracy \emph{and} loses a smaller fraction of what it solves, with conditional fooling of $48.58$ versus $55.53\%$ under PGD-10 and $55.78$ versus $60.37\%$ under AutoAttack. On the $n=7{,}061\pm68$ samples both models classify correctly on clean inputs, an exactly paired comparison, the CNN leads by 13.8 and 10.7 points (McNemar $p<10^{-67}$ in every run; the bound is computed on this subset and is weaker than the full-test-set bound quoted above, which is what the larger denominator buys).

The decomposition is worth reporting even when the two factors agree, because they need not, and because their relative weight is less stable than the ordering they produce. Our first checkpoints, selected with a validation split that clean pre-training had already seen, gave the same absolute ordering but the opposite conditional one: fooling rates that were indistinguishable under PGD-10 ($52.15$ versus $52.33\%$) and slightly ViT-favorable under AutoAttack ($58.16$ versus $56.46\%$), supporting the reading that the CNN advantage was entirely a clean-accuracy effect (lower block of Table~\ref{tab:conditional}). We cannot isolate a cause, since the corrected protocol changed validation handling and the training run together; a data-quantity explanation does not hold, because the corrected clean models are \emph{better} despite seeing 2{,}000 fewer images ($80.09\pm0.60$ versus $77.50\%$ for the ViT). The defensible conclusion is narrower and more useful than a mechanism: an attribution of this kind should not be drawn from a single training run, and a paper that reports only the absolute score hides which factor is doing the work.

\subsection{Transfer Attack Analysis}
\label{subsec:transfer}

This section contains the paper's central measurement. We craft PGD-10 adversarial examples on a source model and evaluate them on a target, on the full test set, for each of the three seed pairs. Because outcomes are logged per sample, the same attack run can be scored under several conditioning protocols without recomputation: the protocols differ only in which samples enter the denominator (Section~\ref{subsec:metrics}).

\centering

\label{tab:transfer_protocols}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{3pt}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Protocol} & \textbf{CNN$\rightarrow$ViT} & \textbf{ViT$\rightarrow$CNN} & \textbf{Diff.} & \textbf{Diff.}$^{\ddagger}$ \\

The denominators differ sharply between protocols. For CNN$\rightarrow$ViT and ViT$\rightarrow$CNN respectively they are 10{,}000/10{,}000 without conditioning, 7{,}353/8{,}579 under target-correct, 7{,}061 shared samples under both-correct, and 2{,}831/3{,}814 under successful-source. The spread in the Diff.\ column is the standard deviation of the paired within-seed difference, which the two per-direction spreads cannot recover because the directions covary across seeds; it is the training-run reference of Section~\ref{subsec:statistics_results}. The protocol, not the model, dominates the measured quantity. On identical models and identical data, the asymmetry ranges from $+4.36\pm0.44$ to $+19.37\pm1.27$ points; the mean per-seed spread between the largest and the smallest protocol estimate is $15.01\pm0.84$ points, a factor of 4.4, whereas the seed-level standard deviation within any single protocol stays below 1.5 points. That spread is not a single quantity, and separating it is part of the result. Take the unconditioned rate as the pivot: the successful-source rate sits $5.79$ points above it and conditions on the source's own white-box failure, so it estimates a different quantity, while the target-correct rate sits $9.21$ points below it and that entire gap is what Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_identity} predicts in closed form, so a reader given the target's clean error can remove it exactly. The two account for the full $15.01$ points. What survives the correction is the difference between the two protocols we endorse for directional claims, target-correct and both-correct, which differ only in whether the source must also be correct: $3.91$ points, still more than twice what retraining both architectures moves. That residual is $5.97$ points on CIFAR-100 and $0.65$ on SVHN. The pivot structure is dataset-dependent: on CIFAR-100 the unconditioned rate is the largest of the four, so the whole $13.58$-point spread there is the identity term and the successful-source rate falls inside it. The protocol also decides the statistical verdict. Under the both-correct protocol, which scores both directions on the same samples and therefore supports paired inference, the difference is $8.27\pm0.24$ points, differing in the third decimal from the $\pm0.23$ of the table only because the two estimators differ. Each run has its own paired bootstrap CI ($[7.61, 9.44]$, $[7.10, 9.01]$ and $[7.28, 9.20]$); we summarize them by the average of their endpoints, $[7.33, 9.22]$, which is a description of three intervals and not itself an interval with a coverage guarantee, and we give the across-run $t$ interval, $[7.68, 8.86]$, for the inference that is actually at the run level. The sign-flip permutation $p$ is below $5\times10^{-5}$ in every seed, and TOST unable to establish equivalence at any of the three margins ($\pm1$, $\pm2$ and $\pm3$ points, $p=1.0$ in each), the interval lying entirely outside $\pm3$; under the target-correct protocol on our earlier checkpoints, the same pipeline accepted equivalence at $\pm$2 points. A reader given only one of these numbers would draw a different conclusion about whether the architectures differ at all.

A protocol name is not by itself a specification. The successful-source protocol conditions on samples whose attack succeeded on the source, and whether that phrase requires the source to classify the clean input correctly is a sub-choice that transfer studies rarely state. We report the stricter reading, in which white-box success means that the source is correct on the clean input and wrong on its adversarial counterpart. Under the looser reading, which also admits samples the source already misclassified, the measured asymmetry falls from $+19.37$ to $+14.60$ points and the protocol spread from $15.01$ to $10.45$ points. That shift of $4.77$ points is larger than the run-to-run standard deviation of any protocol in Table~\ref{tab:transfer_protocols} by more than a factor of three, and the other three protocols are unchanged by construction. Because a sub-choice of this size is invisible in a protocol's name, all four conditioning masks are defined in a single place in the released code.

What is stable is the direction: CNN-crafted examples transfer better to the ViT than the reverse under all four protocols, in all three seeds, and also under the momentum-based MI-FGSM~\cite{dong2018boosting} ($19.07\pm0.33$ versus $15.42\pm0.52\%$ target-correct; this bidirectional check uses the attack's own default step of $\alpha=\eps/10$, not the $\alpha=2/255$ used elsewhere, which is why its ViT$\rightarrow$CNN figure differs slightly from the matched-budget value in Table~\ref{tab:tgr}), so the effect is not an artifact of the PGD attack itself. The magnitude, its significance, and any equivalence claim are protocol-dependent; the sign is not.

The mechanism behind the spread is compositional. Samples that the target solves on clean input but the source does not are three to four times more fragile than jointly solved ones ($41.2\pm0.8\%$ and $59.1\pm1.7\%$ fooling versus $10.0$ and $18.3\%$), and these marginal subsets differ five-fold in size ($1{,}517\pm102$ versus $291\pm16$) because the two models differ in clean accuracy. Conditioning on target-correct admits both marginal subsets, whose asymmetric sizes pull the two rates toward each other; conditioning on both-correct excludes them, and the directional difference reappears. This account covers three of the four protocols. The successful-source protocol is driven differently, and separating the two drivers changes what the spread depends on: across $18$ direction pairs drawn from six seed-and-dataset clusters, the spread of the four-protocol asymmetry \emph{falls} as the clean-error gap between the two models widens (slope $-0.528$, cluster-bootstrap CI $[-0.664, -0.418]$), whereas the spread computed without the successful-source protocol \emph{rises} with it (slope $+0.431$, CI $[+0.333, +0.633]$). The successful-source rate is the extreme of the four in $16$ of the $18$ pairs, and the widest pair is target-correct against successful-source ($23.77$ points average gap, widest in $13$ of the $18$ directions). Conditioning on source success selects by the \emph{source's} own robustness, which is not a function of the pair's clean-accuracy gap, so it contributes a second and partly opposing term. The pool for this fit excludes the SVHN pair, and we record how that exclusion arose because it is not what the pre-registration says. The registration placed SVHN in the fitted arm and named it as the pair that would occupy the empty band below twelve points on the $x$ axis; its reassignment to an independent consistency check was written after the SVHN result was in hand. We therefore treat the SVHN-inclusive fit as the pre-registered one. Adding its two directions moves the four-protocol slope to $-0.133$, with a cluster-bootstrap CI of $[-0.570, +0.469]$ that includes zero, while the three-protocol slope keeps its sign and its interval ($+0.470$, CI $[+0.361, +0.665]$). We therefore withdraw the four-protocol slope; the mechanism claim rests on the three-protocol one, which survives the pool question and is what Section~\ref{subsec:statistics_results} carries forward.

We calibrate the protocol spread against the clean-error gap in two ways that fail differently, and they agree on three of the four protocols. The \emph{controlled} arm holds one member of a pair fixed and sweeps the other along its own training trajectory, so architecture, seed, dataset, and attack budget stay constant while the gap moves ($116$ points from eight seed-and-dataset clusters). The \emph{observational} arm compares final models across architectures, seeds, and datasets, where everything moves at once ($18$ direction pairs from six clusters). Inference in both is by cluster bootstrap at the trajectory level; the degrees of freedom are set by the number of clusters, not by the number of points, and we do not pool the arms. Two cautions apply to these intervals. With six to eight clusters a percentile cluster bootstrap undercovers, so the intervals below should be read as lower bounds on width, not as exact. And the WideResNet is a single fixed external checkpoint that appears in all six observational clusters, in twelve of the eighteen direction pairs, so those clusters are not fully independent; the pair-only sensitivity that excludes it is reported alongside.

Excluding the successful-source protocol, the two arms agree: $+0.673$ (CI $[+0.602, +0.727]$) in the controlled arm against $+0.470$ (CI $[+0.361, +0.665]$) in the observational arm on the pre-registered pool, same sign and overlapping intervals. The four-protocol slope, which we withdrew above, supports no comparison either way: on the pre-registered pool the observational interval $[-0.570, +0.469]$ contains the controlled arm's $+0.273$ (CI $[+0.219, +0.371]$) entirely, so the arms neither agree nor disagree there in any informative sense. We record the post-hoc contrast only to show what the pool choice does. Dropping SVHN moves the observational four-protocol slope to $-0.528$ (CI $[-0.664, -0.418]$), which does not overlap the controlled arm, and restricting that pool to the matched architecture pair moves it to $-0.100$ (CI $[-0.464, +0.092]$); a quantity this sensitive to which pairs enter the fit is not one a mechanism claim can rest on. The agreement that does carry the claim is the three-protocol one, and it holds in every pool we fit. Two limits belong with this: the observational arm's negative slope is fitted across an unmeasured gap of $7.2$ points on the $x$ axis, so it compares two separated clusters instead of tracing a trend; and the controlled arm's four-protocol slope keeps its sign but not its magnitude when early checkpoints are dropped, moving over $+0.201$ to $+0.492$ across thresholds, a range its own interval of $[+0.219, +0.371]$ does not cover; the three-protocol slope is the most stable quantity of all ($0.661$ to $0.673$).

\subsubsection{How much of the raw rate is the target's clean error?}
\label{subsubsec:matrix}

To quantify the confound directly instead of arguing it, we extend the analysis to a $3\times3$ matrix by adding the WideResNet-28-10 reference as both source and target (Table~\ref{tab:transfer_matrix}). The relationship between the two rates turns out to be stronger than a correlation: it is an identity resting on a single empirical fact.

Write $e$ for the target's clean error, $r_{\text{raw}}$ for the unconditioned rate and $r_{\text{cond}}$ for the target-correct conditioned rate. If a sample the target already misclassifies on clean input is still misclassified after the perturbation, then $r_{\text{raw}} = e + r_{\text{cond}}(1-e)$, and therefore
\begin{equation}
r_{\text{raw}} - r_{\text{cond}} = e\,(1 - r_{\text{cond}}).
\label{eq:raw_identity}
\end{equation}

The antecedent is the part that is measured, and it holds tightly: across $36$ off-diagonal directions (two datasets, three seed pairs, three targets, two sources) the probability that a clean-misclassified sample is also misclassified under attack ranges from $0.989$ to $1.000$. Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_identity} consequently reproduces every measured unconditioned rate to within $0.41$ points, with a mean absolute residual of $0.095$ points; the residual accounts for at most $1.39\%$ of the deviation being explained.

This changes the status of the claim, and strengthens it. The deviation of unconditioned from conditioned transfer rates does not merely correlate with the target's clean error in our data; it is that error, scaled by $(1 - r_{\text{cond}})$, up to a residual below half a point. Unconditioned transfer rates are contaminated by target weakness \emph{by construction}, in any study whose targets satisfy the antecedent, not as a regularity we happened to observe on three targets. The antecedent is itself a measurable quantity and should be measured rather than assumed. Writing $p$ for the probability that a clean-misclassified sample stays misclassified under attack, the general form is $r_{\text{raw}} = e\,p + r_{\text{cond}}\,(1 - e)$, which reduces to Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_identity} when $p=1$. Where $p<1$, Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_identity} overstates the deviation by exactly $e\,(1-p)$, so it is an upper bound and not an equality once the antecedent fails. We measure $p$ between $0.989$ and $1.000$ on adversarially trained targets, where the correction is therefore exact to within a few tenths of a point. On standard-trained targets, which is the regime most transfer studies attack, we observed $p$ as low as $0.88$ in our own pre-correction runs; we quote that as an order-of-magnitude indication and not as a measured value of the present pipeline, and there the identity is a bound. The algebra itself is not new. Written over a single model instead of a source-target pair, $r_{\text{raw}} = e + r_{\text{cond}}(1 - e)$ is the natural-plus-boundary decomposition of TRADES~\cite{zhang2019theoretically}, where the premise corresponding to our $p$ holds by definition because the boundary term is defined by an existential quantifier over the perturbation neighborhood. What is new here is that in the transfer setting the premise is not definitional: a fixed source-crafted perturbation either preserves the target's clean error or does not, so $p$ becomes an empirical quantity, and we measure it rather than assume it. We previously reported this as a Pearson correlation ($r = 0.997$), which understated it: a near-unit correlation between a quantity and its own dominant component is arithmetic, and the small-sample caveat it required is unnecessary once the identity is written down.

What remains dataset-specific is the proportionality factor, not the mechanism. Because $(1 - r_{\text{cond}})$ depends on how transferable the attack is, the fitted slope is $0.762$ points per point of target error on CIFAR-10 and $0.656$ on CIFAR-100. It is an average over the targets measured, not a constant, and it should be re-estimated, not assumed, when the identity is applied to other model pools. Unconditioned transfer rates therefore do not measure transferability across architectures; they measure transferability plus the target's clean weakness, in a proportion that Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_identity} makes explicit.

\subsection{Does the Protocol Effect Generalize? A Second Dataset}
\label{subsec:cifar100}

The measurement above is made on one dataset, and a protocol effect that exists only on CIFAR-10 would not support a general reporting recommendation. We therefore repeated the whole design on CIFAR-100: same architectures, same threat model, same attack budget, same three-seed structure, same four protocols. Before running it we registered three directional predictions and three endpoint bands in an append-only document, so that the outcome could not be reinterpreted after the fact.

\label{tab:cifar100_results}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lccc}

\midrule

\bottomrule

Across the same four protocols the measured CNN$\rightarrow$ViT asymmetry runs from $+4.96\pm1.01$ points (target-correct) to $+18.53\pm0.71$ points (unconditioned), with $+10.92\pm0.34$ under both-correct and $+17.50\pm0.92$ under successful-source. The mean per-seed spread is $13.83\pm1.30$ points, against $15.01\pm0.84$ on CIFAR-10, so the harder dataset leaves the protocol effect in the same range, slightly below the CIFAR-10 value instead of above it. The direction is positive in all twelve measurements (four protocols $\times$ three seed pairs), as it was on CIFAR-10. Under the both-correct protocol the paired difference is $10.92$ points with a bootstrap CI of $[9.47, 12.35]$, a sign-flip permutation $p$ below the resolution of $20{,}000$ permutations in every seed, and equivalence rejected at every margin tested.

One of the three registered predictions is confirmed, one fails under the reading we treat as binding, and the third is not testable in this design. The prediction that the sign would be preserved is confirmed ($12/12$). The prediction that the protocol spread would be larger on the harder dataset was registered as a comparison against the CIFAR-10 spread, quoted at the time as $10.45$ points. Against that literal threshold it holds ($13.83 > 10.45$); against the CIFAR-10 spread as it now stands under the successful-source definition of Section~\ref{subsec:transfer} it does not ($13.83 < 15.01$). We report the failure rather than the reading that would rescue it, because the registration named the CIFAR-10 spread and not the number. The two datasets place the protocol effect in the same range instead of ordering it by difficulty, so the mechanism we propose predicts the size of the effect but not its ordering between these two datasets. The third predicted that the deviation of the unconditioned from the conditioned rate would grow with the target's clean error. The measured slope is $+0.656$ points per point of target error, positive as predicted; but on CIFAR-100 two of the three targets have clean errors that differ by $0.21$ points ($36.15$ and $36.36$), so the correlation is computed over two effective clusters, not three, and its value ($r = 0.931$ over six directions, $0.974$ aggregated to targets) rests on a two-point contrast. The measurement is \emph{consistent with} the prediction and does not \emph{test} it; we report it as such and not as a third confirmation.

One registered endpoint was missed: we predicted clean accuracy in the $33$ to $40\%$ band for the adversarially trained ViT and measured $43.17\pm1.10\%$. The band was derived by assuming that the clean-accuracy cost of adversarial training scales the same way for both architectures. It does not: the ViT retained $0.885$ of its clean pre-training accuracy under adversarial training, against $0.802$ for the ResNet. The two adversarial endpoints, which are the ones the protocol claim depends on, fell inside their bands (PGD-10 predicted $10$ to $14\%$, measured $11.15$; AutoAttack predicted $8$ to $11\%$, measured $8.87$).

Because conditioning changes which samples enter the denominator, it also changes the class mix, so an asymmetry could in principle be a composition artifact; it is not. We decompose each direction's rate into a composition component and a rate component. Under target-correct the composition effect is $-1.249$, $-1.330$ and $-1.065$ points across the three seed pairs, that is $17.4$ to $29.7\%$ of the asymmetry and \emph{negative}: rebalancing the class mix would make the asymmetry larger, not smaller. Under successful-source it is small and positive in every seed ($+0.527$, $+0.435$ and $+0.382$ points, that is $2.1$ to $3.1\%$ of the asymmetry). Under the unconditioned and both-correct protocols it is exactly zero, which is the arithmetic check the decomposition must pass.

Two qualifications belong with this result. The two adversarial bands held with margins of $0.22$ points (AutoAttack) and $0.48$ points (PGD-10) at the nearest seed-level edge. Those margins are smaller than the checkpoint-selection noise on this same dataset: sweeping early-stopping patience and curve smoothing over the CIFAR-100 trajectories, with the training run held fixed, moves the reported PGD-10 accuracy by $0.18$ to $1.83$ points (mean $0.86$). The same nine-cell sweep on CIFAR-10 gives $0.54$ to $2.83$ points per validation split, and the eighteen-cell figure quoted in Section~\ref{subsec:statistics_results} is larger still because it also varies the split, a dimension the CIFAR-100 protocol does not have. The bands held, but by margins at or below the selection noise of the dataset they were registered for; a reader should weigh the pre-registration accordingly. Second, the same held-out split serves selection at both stages instead of two independent splits. That choice is inherited deliberately from the CIFAR-10 protocol so the two datasets remain comparable, and Section~\ref{subsec:statistics_results} reports what it does and does not cost.

\subsection{A Third Dataset Where the Architectures Are Matched}
\label{subsec:svhn}

CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 both place the two architectures far apart in clean accuracy (about $12$ and $21$ points). SVHN~\cite{netzer2011reading} does not (Table~\ref{tab:cifar100_results} reports the CIFAR-100 absolutes for comparison): adversarially trained under the same recipe, the ResNet reaches $94.27\pm0.70\%$ clean and the ViT $92.42\pm0.56\%$, a gap of $1.85$ points. Robust accuracies are $55.52\pm0.64$ and $52.30\pm0.10$ under PGD-10. This is the regime our thesis makes the sharpest prediction about, and the prediction is not the one a reader of the previous sections would expect.

When the architectures are matched, the protocol decides the sign. Two of the four protocols report a CNN advantage and two report a ViT advantage (Table~\ref{tab:svhn_protocols}). The split is not arbitrary. The two protocols we endorse for directional claims, target-correct and both-correct, agree with each other and both favor the ViT; the sign change separates them from the unconditioned rate and the successful-source rate. The unconditioned reversal is moreover the arithmetic consequence of Equation~\eqref{eq:raw_identity}: the ViT target's clean error exceeds the CNN's by about two points here, which is enough to carry a $-1.00$ conditioned difference to $+0.39$ raw. Reporting a raw rate can therefore flip the sign of a published comparison, and the identity says in advance when it will. Under the both-correct protocol, which we treat as primary because it scores both directions on identical samples, the difference is $-0.35$ points with a paired bootstrap CI of $[-0.76, +0.07]$, a sign-flip permutation $p$ of $0.105$, and TOST equivalence \emph{accepted} at all three margins, including the tightest one we test ($\pm1$ point, $p=0.0007$). A study reporting only the successful-source rate would announce a $2.70$-point CNN advantage on SVHN; one reporting only the target-correct rate would announce a $1.00$-point ViT advantage. Both would be arithmetically correct and neither would be reproducible by the other.

This behavior is consistent with the mechanism, not an exception to it. The protocol spread on SVHN is $3.70\pm0.62$ points, against $15.01\pm0.84$ on CIFAR-10 and $13.83\pm1.30$ on CIFAR-100, roughly four times smaller than the two CIFAR values while the clean-accuracy gaps are $1.85$, $\approx12$ and $\approx21$ points. The relation is not monotone across the three: the largest gap does not produce the largest spread, and CIFAR-100 sits slightly below CIFAR-10. What separates the datasets here is the near-matched pair, not the ordering among the mismatched ones. Once the spread is small enough it no longer clears the asymmetry itself, so the sign becomes a property of the protocol and not of the architectures. The direction we reported as stable in Section~\ref{subsec:transfer} is stable \emph{in the regime where the two models differ in clean accuracy}, which is the regime both CIFAR datasets happen to occupy.

SVHN was run in a reduced configuration fixed in advance: two seed pairs instead of three, $50$ adversarial-training epochs, no AutoAttack, and no third architecture. The last of these means the $3\times3$ confound control of Section~\ref{subsubsec:matrix} cannot be built here, and we do not build a within-SVHN version of it.

The contrast with the conditional decomposition of Section~\ref{subsec:robustness} is therefore about direction, not magnitude: there the attribution between clean accuracy and conditional sensitivity changed between checkpoint sets, whereas here no gradient-structure difference changes sign.

\subsubsection{Sparsity is not spatial locality}
\label{subsubsec:spatial}

Sparsity measures answer how \emph{many} gradient components are large, not whether those components sit together in the image. The distinction matters because the sparser-CNN result invites a spatial reading, in which gradients are taken to be more ``localized'' or ``concentrated'' on object regions. We do not attribute that reading to a particular study; we tested it because it is the natural interpretation of a sparsity result and because our own earlier figures encouraged it. The measures do not support it. We therefore measure spatial structure directly on the same gradients, using four scale-invariant descriptors of the channel-summed energy map: the smallest pixel fraction carrying 50\% and 90\% of the gradient energy, the spatial entropy of the normalized energy map, and Moran's $I$ with 4-neighborhood weights, which is positive when energy clusters in adjacent pixels.

Two results follow. The architectures differ less in \emph{whether} damage accumulates than in \emph{where}. In the ViT the drift is gradual and shallow: similarity declines monotonically to a minimum at block 11 and recovers slightly at the output, while feature norms stay within $0.45\%$ of their clean values throughout, so the perturbation rotates the representation instead of rescaling it. In the CNN the damage is concentrated: the first three stages lose little, then the first block of the last residual stage collapses to $0.8777\pm0.0181$ together with a $-13.14\pm1.89\%$ norm contraction, after which the final block partially repairs the representation ($0.9108\pm0.0060$, norm $+5.84\pm3.53\%$). Adversarial damage in the CNN is thus a late, localized, magnitude-changing event; in the ViT it is a distributed, direction-only rotation. We note that on our earlier checkpoints the CNN profile fell monotonically to the last block, with no recovery, so the shape of the CNN curve, unlike the ViT profile, should be read as recipe-dependent.

Second, the aggregation choice changes the measured profile substantially. At their respective minima the CLS pathway drifts about three times as far as the patch-token mean ($0.9604$ versus $0.9870$, a ratio of $3.05$ in $1-\cos$), and the two summaries place the minimum at different depths (block 11 versus block 9). Since the CLS token at the block output is what the classifier reads, this is not a nuisance but a result: the perturbation concentrates on the classification pathway while the average patch representation is comparatively preserved. The tensor the summary is taken from matters as much as the aggregation. Read at the MLP sub-block output instead of the residual block output, the same three summaries give a CLS minimum of $0.9343$ at block 10, a patch-token minimum of $0.9840$ at block 8 and a ratio of $4.12$: the drift looks larger, the depths reorder, and the sub-block is not the tensor the classifier reads. Layer-wise drift curves from different papers are therefore not comparable unless both the aggregation and the tensor are stated.

Table~\ref{tab:feature_degradation} reports the block-wise profiles. A third measurement is negative. Adversarial perturbation does not measurably change the entropy of the CLS attention distribution: over $n=1000$ samples per run, the mean per-layer change is at most $0.005$ nats in absolute value against layer means near 4.9 nats, with a seed-level standard deviation of the same order. We state this as equivalence rather than as a failure to reject: with a margin of $\pm 0.05$ nats, one percent of the layer mean and fixed from the scale of the measure and not from the data, TOST accepts equivalence in all twelve layers, the weakest at $p=0.006$. What does grow with depth is the \emph{displacement} of the attention map, from $0.028$ total variation in the first layer to $0.084$ in the last, small in absolute terms, since total variation is bounded by 1. The attack therefore redirects attention slightly without broadening or sharpening it; Figure~\ref{fig:feature_degradation} plots the per-layer curves, which lie on top of each other. We flag this explicitly because an earlier version of this analysis, computed on an 8-sample figure batch, suggested a visible attention effect that the larger measurement does not support; any claim that adversarial attack degrades attention should be checked at this sample size before being made. We raise this against our own preliminary version rather than against a specific study in the literature.

Finally, drift magnitude does not predict attack success. Splitting the samples by whether the attack flipped the prediction, the last-block cosine similarity of flipped and unflipped ViT samples is statistically indistinguishable ($0.9895$ versus $0.9877$); for the CNN there is a small difference in the expected direction ($0.9058$ versus $0.9143$). Feature drift is therefore a description of how the representation moves, not a sufficient statistic for whether the prediction changes.

Penultimate-space geometry is measured in the original feature space, not from a low-dimensional embedding, which would invite qualitative over-reading ($n$=500 per run, white-box, mean$\pm$std over three runs). The measurable statement is therefore that the CNN's well-separated clean feature space is heavily disrupted by white-box attack, whereas the ViT's class structure is weaker to begin with and moves less in these aggregate terms; this is a statement about geometry, not about robustness, since the CNN nonetheless retains the higher robust accuracy.

\subsection{Does a ViT-Specific Transfer Attack Change the Picture?}
\label{subsec:tgr}

All transfer results so far use gradient attacks that treat the ViT as a generic differentiable model. A natural objection is that the ViT direction is handicapped by this choice, since attacks designed for transformer structure are reported to transfer better. We test the objection directly with Token Gradient Regularization (TGR)~\cite{zhang2023tgr}, which suppresses extreme-valued token gradients in the attention, QKV, and MLP paths during backpropagation on the source model. Our implementation is an adaptation: the original targets standardly trained ImageNet ViTs, whereas we apply the same mechanism to our CIFAR-10 ViT-Tiny wrapper at the budget used throughout this paper (Section~\ref{subsec:attacks}), with plain MI-FGSM generated in the same run as a paired control.

TGR does not improve transfer in this regime; it degrades it (Table~\ref{tab:tgr}). The regularization behaves as designed on the source side, trading away 14.9 points of white-box strength, but the trade does not pay off at the target: conditioned transfer falls by 3.0 points under the target-correct protocol and by 2.2 under both-correct. Paired McNemar tests on the both-correct subset favor MI-FGSM decisively in every run ($p = 6.1\times10^{-10}$, $1.1\times10^{-27}$, $7.0\times10^{-34}$), so this is not a seed effect.

We state the conclusion narrowly, because our setting differs from the one TGR was designed for in three respects that could each matter: the targets are adversarially trained, not standardly trained, the data are $32\times32$ CIFAR-10 images upsampled inside the model instead of native ImageNet, and the source is a 5.7M-parameter ViT-Tiny. The claim we can support is that TGR's transfer advantage does not carry over to this regime, not that the method fails in general. For the purposes of this paper the relevant consequence is that the CNN$\rightarrow$ViT asymmetry reported in Section~\ref{subsec:transfer} is not an artifact of using architecture-agnostic attacks: giving the ViT direction a transformer-specific attack makes it weaker, not stronger.

\subsection{Sources of Variance}
\label{subsec:statistics_results}

Four sources of variance bear on the comparisons above. Attack-initialization stochasticity is negligible: re-running PGD-10 with three attack seeds (42, 123, 456) on a fixed 1{,}000-sample subset leaves clean accuracy exactly unchanged, since the loader does not shuffle, and moves robust accuracy by a standard deviation of $0.05$ points for the CNN and $0.00$ for the ViT. Training-run variance is larger but still small: across the three independent runs per architecture, the standard deviation for the adversarially trained pair is at most $0.55$ points on clean accuracy and $0.50$ on PGD-10 (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}).

Comparing the protocol effect against those two figures directly would be an error of scale, because the protocol effect is measured on the transfer asymmetry while both figures above are measured on absolute accuracy. We therefore quantify the protocol and training-run effects on the \emph{same} quantity, the off-diagonal asymmetry, and report both in absolute units. Within a fixed seed pair, changing only the conditioning protocol moves the asymmetry by $15.01\pm0.84$ points. This figure is the mean of the per-seed ranges; it coincides with the range of the protocol means here because the extremal protocols are the same in all three seeds, and differs on CIFAR-100 ($13.83$ against $13.58$) because there they are not. Retraining both architectures from scratch moves that same asymmetry by a standard deviation of only $0.23$ to $1.27$ points, the value depending on which protocol it is measured under (Table~\ref{tab:transfer_protocols}, Diff.\ column). A fifteen-point measurement effect against a sub-$1.5$-point training effect is the ordering the paper rests on.

We deliberately do not headline the ratio of these two spreads. With three runs the denominator carries two degrees of freedom, so a $\chi^2$ interval on any single ratio is very wide: for the successful-source protocol it runs from $0.8$ to $9.9$ and includes unity. Reported as a sensitivity, the ratio spans $5.2$ to $32.6$ across dispersion measures and reference protocols, and equals $28.3$ under the like-for-like comparison on the both-correct protocol we treat as primary. Two cautions bound it further: the protocol with the largest asymmetry also carries the largest run-to-run standard deviation, so numerator and denominator are not independent, and because all three runs share one validation split the denominator excludes split-to-split variance, which makes the ratio an upper bound. The absolute comparison survives both, which is why it and not the fold-change carries the claim. This ordering is the quantitative form of the paper's argument: with a protocol left unstated, a reader cannot tell a real architectural difference from a measurement choice.

A fourth source is the offline checkpoint-selection protocol, which is usually left implicit. Holding a training trajectory fixed and varying only how its final checkpoint is chosen (validation split, early-stopping patience, and whether the validation curve is smoothed before the argmax; eighteen combinations) moves the reported PGD-10 accuracy by $2.62$ to $2.85$ points per seed for the ResNet and $1.58$ to $2.09$ points for the ViT. The weights on disk are identical across these cells; only the rule that picks one of them differs. About half of that dispersion comes from one of the three dimensions. Smoothing the validation curve before the argmax lowers test PGD-10 accuracy monotonically in every ResNet run and in two of the three ViT runs, so it is not a neutral alternative but a systematically worse rule; restricted to the split and patience choices that studies actually vary, the dispersion falls to $1.09$ times the seed-level standard deviation for the ResNet and $1.42$ times for the ViT. We report both: the larger measures what an undocumented preprocessing step can add, the smaller what a documented protocol still leaves open. The dispersion is quoted in absolute units and deliberately not as a ratio to seed-level dispersion, because that ratio depends on which of the eighteen cells is the reference: across all eighteen it runs from $0.67$ to $3.38$ for the ResNet and $0.63$ to $7.38$ for the ViT, inverting the comparison for some of them.

A counterweight belongs immediately next to it: on CIFAR-100 the epoch selected by our own protocol varies by $25$ epochs across the three ResNet seeds, yet the resulting test PGD-10 standard deviation is $0.32$ points. The selection \emph{path} was unstable while the selected \emph{outcome} was not. Neither observation generalizes without the other: selection choices can move a reported number by more than a training seed does, and they can also leave it nearly untouched, and which of the two happens is a property of the trajectory's plateau and not of the architecture. The practical consequence for reporting is the same in both cases: the selection rule is part of the result and belongs in the methods section.

\textbf{Computational Details:} The training runs and analyses reported here were conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 5090 (32GB VRAM); the earlier single-run checkpoints used for the comparison in Table~\ref{tab:conditional} were trained on an RTX 5060 Ti (16GB). All runs used PyTorch 2.6.0 with CUDA 12.8 and deterministic cuDNN settings. AutoAttack evaluation used the standard configuration (APGD-CE, APGD-T, FAB-T, Square) on the full 10{,}000-sample test set in seeded 1{,}000-sample chunks with per-sample outcome logging. The three-seed training pipeline required 21.7 GPU-hours in total.
