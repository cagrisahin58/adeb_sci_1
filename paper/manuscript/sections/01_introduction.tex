\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Deep neural networks are vulnerable to adversarial examples, carefully crafted perturbations that cause confident misclassification while remaining bounded by a small norm budget~\cite{szegedy2014intriguing, goodfellow2015explaining}. As Vision Transformers (ViTs)~\cite{dosovitskiy2021image, vaswani2017attention} joined convolutional neural networks (CNNs)~\cite{he2016deep} as a dominant architecture family, a natural question followed: which family is more robust, and why?

The literature answers this question inconsistently: Bai et al.~\cite{bai2021transformers} report that under matched training recipes transformers do not surpass CNNs, whereas Benz et al.~\cite{benz2021adversarial} report ViTs to be more robust for standardly trained models. Transfer studies add a disagreement of their own: several report that attacks move more easily from ViTs to CNNs than the reverse~\cite{mahmood2021robustness, benz2021adversarial, shao2022adversarial}, whereas Naseer et al.~\cite{naseer2022improving} find that perturbations crafted on ViTs transfer poorly under conventional attacks, and the direction measured in this paper is the opposite of the first group's, a point Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to. These disagreements are usually attributed to differences in models, training recipes, or datasets, and the standard response is to compare under a matched recipe.

Yet even when the models, the data, the threat model, and the attack budget are all held fixed, the conclusion still depends on a choice that is rarely reported: the \emph{measurement protocol}. Transfer studies must decide which samples enter the denominator (all test samples, those the target classifies correctly, those both models classify correctly, or those on which the source attack succeeded), and robustness comparisons must decide whether to report an absolute score or to separate it into clean accuracy and conditional susceptibility. Papers usually treat these choices as reporting details, but they are part of the result.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Approach
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
The setting is kept small so that the measurement question can be isolated. We adversarially train ResNet-18 and ViT-Tiny on CIFAR-10~\cite{krizhevsky2009learning} under a matched objective and threat model, with three independent training runs per architecture and a fixed validation split held out before \emph{any} training stage, so that no model is selected on data it has seen. All evaluations run on the full test set with per-sample outcome logging, which makes paired statistics possible: the same samples can be re-weighted under different protocols without re-running an attack. With the models fixed, only the protocol is varied, and we measure how far the conclusion moves.

Architecture comparisons also invite three readings that we treat as measurable hypotheses: that CNN input gradients are spatially more localized, that adversarial perturbation degrades ViT attention, and that ViT-specific transfer attacks improve cross-architecture transfer. Only the last is a published claim; the first two are interpretations that a sparsity result or a small batch of attention figures readily suggests, our own earlier figures included. Each is tested directly with a purpose-built measurement.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
% Contributions
% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
Our first contribution is to quantify protocol sensitivity. On identical models and data, the measured cross-architecture transfer asymmetry ranges from $+4.4$ to $+19.4$ points across four conditioning protocols, whereas retraining both architectures from scratch moves the same quantity by under $1.5$ points. The spread decomposes as follows. The $9.21$ points that separate the unconditioned estimate from the target-correct estimate are removed in closed form by an identity we derive, and $5.79$ points come from a protocol that estimates a different quantity. After the correction, $3.91$ points of discretion remain between two protocols that are both defensible, still more than twice the retraining effect. The protocol also decides whether an equivalence test is accepted or rejected. The direction of the asymmetry, unlike its magnitude, is stable \emph{as long as the two models differ in clean accuracy}; once they are matched, two of the four protocols reverse it. A full replication on CIFAR-100 leaves the spread close to its CIFAR-10 size and preserves its sign, and the offline checkpoint-selection rule, varied on a fixed training trajectory, turns out to be a source of variance that papers rarely report.

The second contribution concerns unconditioned transfer rates, which are confounded by the target's clean error. In a $3\times3$ transfer matrix that adds a WideResNet-28-10 reference, the deviation of raw from conditioned rates obeys an identity. Because samples the target already misclassifies are essentially never rescued by the perturbation ($0.989$ to $1.000$ over $36$ directions), the raw rate equals the clean error plus the conditioned rate scaled by one minus that error, to within $0.41$ points. Incoming transfer also tracks the target's own vulnerability ($r = 0.986$ across three targets), which reframes the asymmetry as a property of the weaker target. Absolute robustness, for its part, factors exactly into clean accuracy and conditional susceptibility. Both factors need to be reported, because the attribution between them changed between our earlier single-run checkpoints and three fresh runs while the absolute ordering did not.

Three of the results we report are negative: scale-invariant sparsity differences do not correspond to spatial localization differences, on all four spatial measures; CLS attention entropy does not change measurably under attack at $n=1000$; and Token Gradient Regularization~\cite{zhang2023tgr}, a ViT-specific transfer attack, gives no transfer gain in this regime, losing to plain MI-FGSM under a matched budget. Two behavioral differences do survive. Gradient sparsity differences reproduce across independent checkpoint sets, are created by adversarial training, and are accompanied by an alignment difference that exists only in absolute cosine. Adversarial damage accumulates at qualitatively different depths in the two architectures, and the measured drift profile depends on the token aggregation used to compute it.

The measurements translate into concrete reporting requirements for cross-architecture robustness studies, which are collected at the end of the discussion.

A preliminary version of this study was submitted to ATEEC 2026~\cite{sahin2026behavioral}. That version reports the conditional decomposition, the four-protocol transfer comparison, the gradient structure measurements and the block-wise drift profiles on CIFAR-10 alone, and describes them as preliminary. The present paper adds two further datasets, a second threat model, the algebraic identity of Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, the checkpoint-selection ablation, the transformer-specific transfer attack, and the pre-registered analysis protocol, and it corrects one claim of the earlier version: the direction of the asymmetry is not stable once the architectures are matched in clean accuracy.
