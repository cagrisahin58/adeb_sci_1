\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Deep neural networks are vulnerable to adversarial examples, carefully crafted perturbations that cause confident misclassification while remaining bounded by a small norm budget~\cite{szegedy2014intriguing, goodfellow2015explaining}. As Vision Transformers (ViTs)~\cite{dosovitskiy2021image, vaswani2017attention} joined convolutional neural networks (CNNs)~\cite{he2016deep} as a dominant architecture family, a natural question followed: which family is more robust, and why?

The literature answers this question inconsistently. Under matched training recipes transformers do not surpass CNNs~\cite{bai2021transformers}, whereas Benz et al.~\cite{benz2021adversarial} find standardly trained ViTs more robust. Transfer studies disagree as well: several report that attacks move more easily from ViTs to CNNs than the reverse~\cite{mahmood2021robustness, benz2021adversarial, shao2022adversarial}, Naseer et al.~\cite{naseer2022improving} find that perturbations crafted on ViTs transfer poorly under conventional attacks, and the direction measured in this paper is the opposite of the first group's, a point Section~\ref{sec:discussion} returns to.

In our adversarially trained pair, examples crafted on the ViT fool the CNN less often than the reverse, and the ViT looks like a weak transfer source. Two different situations produce this pattern. The ViT may craft examples that carry less of what misleads other models, which would be a property of the source. Alternatively, the ViT and the CNNs may simply be dissimilar, so that transfer is low in both directions and the difference between directions comes from how vulnerable each target is. A study with one CNN and one ViT cannot separate the two, because its transfer matrix holds a single asymmetry and an additive split of it into source and target terms is not identified (Proposition~\ref{prop:identifiability}). Separating them requires models outside the pair.

We train ResNet-18 and ViT-Tiny adversarially on CIFAR-10~\cite{krizhevsky2009learning}, three runs each with a validation split fixed before training and per-sample logging over the full test set, and measure both against a fixed panel of seven robust CNNs from RobustBench~\cite{croce2021robustbench}. Each log target-correct transfer rate is split into the target's white-box vulnerability $w_j$, a symmetric pairwise similarity $\simt_{ij}$ and a directional residual $r_{ij}$, and the last two are averaged over the panel into a model's similarity $\sigma$ and direction $\rho$ (Section~\ref{subsec:decomposition_def}). Six new runs complete a two-by-two design that crosses architecture with initialization, random or ImageNet, at a matched input pipeline and training recipe (Section~\ref{subsec:design}). Further blocks test the attack side with attacks selected per source, check for gradient masking with AutoAttack, replicate the panel tests on CIFAR-100, describe the same quantities for independently trained ImageNet models evaluated on ImageNetV2~\cite{recht2019imagenet}, and ask whether input-gradient alignment~\cite{zhang2024why} predicts pairwise similarity. Predictions, tests and decision rules for these blocks were registered before they were run, and Section~\ref{subsec:prereg} lists the data seen beforehand.

The first contribution is an identifiability result. In a two-model design the asymmetry identifies only a difference of potentials, and in a family with a single ViT an additive source-plus-target fit loads the within-family similarity of the CNNs onto the ViT's source and target terms at once, so a source effect read from such a fit may be a similarity effect under another name. A fixed external panel combined with a multiplicative decomposition makes vulnerability, similarity and direction identifiable for each model (Proposition~\ref{prop:identifiability} and Section~\ref{subsec:pair_decomposition}).

The decomposition and the sizes it measures form the second contribution. The relation $\log T_{ij} = \log w_j + \log\simt_{ij} + \tfrac{1}{2} r_{ij}$ is an accounting identity, related to the quasi-symmetry model of Caussinus~\cite{caussinus1965contribution} and to the role Demontis et al.~\cite{demontis2019adversarial} give to target vulnerability, and its content lies in the values its terms take. On CIFAR-10 a share of $0.936$ of ViT-Tiny's panel source gap relative to ResNet-18 is symmetric, the ViT's directional term $\rho = +0.012$ lies within the registered equivalence bound of $\pm0.10$, and ViT seeds resemble each other more than they resemble the panel ($\Dseed = +0.566$). On CIFAR-100 the symmetric share is $0.840$ and the seed similarity replicates, while the ViT's directional term rises to $+0.143$ and the registered equivalence is refuted (Section~\ref{subsec:panel}).

Third, we intervene on the pair itself. Trained from scratch on the same input pipeline, the ViT is less similar to the panel than the ResNet ($\Asig{scr} = -0.364$), ImageNet initialization raises the ViT's panel similarity more than the ResNet's (interaction $\Isig = +0.260$), and the effect of architecture on the directional term is equivalent to zero ($\Arho = +0.003$). All three registered tests are supported after Holm correction (Section~\ref{subsec:twobytwo}). Momentum PGD and our adaptation of token gradient regularization~\cite{zhang2023tgr} do not close the source gap either: under each source's best permitted attack among these and PGD-10, a fraction of $0.960$ of the gap is retained (Section~\ref{subsec:attack_side}), and AutoAttack flags none of the new models for gradient masking.

The fourth contribution concerns the measurement layer underneath. The conditioning protocol, that is, which samples enter the denominator of a transfer rate, moves the asymmetry of the same models from $+4.36$ to $+19.37$ points across four protocols (Section~\ref{subsec:transfer}). Unconditioned and target-correct rates are linked by the law of total probability through the probability $p$ that a sample the target already misclassifies stays misclassified, which is at least $0.989$ on our adversarially trained targets. On the scale $h = -\log(1-T)$ the two protocols then differ only in the target potentials, up to an adjustment term no larger than $0.0044$ in our model pool, so source terms and pairwise similarity on that scale do not depend on the choice between them (Proposition~\ref{prop:hscale}).

These results hold within a definite boundary. The small directional term is a finding on the log scale, against a CNN panel and on CIFAR-10: on the logit scale the ViT's directional term is large and negative, on CIFAR-100 the equivalence fails, and against a single transformer reference the scratch-trained ViT is a directionally weaker source. The architecture contrast compares whole packages, patch stem, attention and parameter count together, and in the scratch row it moves with a lower clean accuracy of the ViT, which this design cannot separate from architecture. The ImageNetV2 panel is descriptive, and its only scratch-trained pair gives an uncertain estimate. Input-gradient alignment, measured at a single clean point, is a necessary condition for similar transfer and is not offered as a mechanism (Sections~\ref{subsec:scale} and~\ref{subsec:limitations}).

Absolute robustness favors the CNN under every attack, with AutoAttack accuracies of $37.93\%$ and $29.14\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:main_results}). Three behavioral readings that such comparisons readily suggest, about gradient locality, attention under attack and transformer-specific attacks, do not hold under direct measurement (Appendix~\ref{app:behavior}).

A preliminary version of this study was submitted to ATEEC 2026~\cite{sahin2026behavioral}. That version reports the conditional decomposition, the four-protocol comparison and the gradient and drift measurements on CIFAR-10 alone. The present paper adds the vulnerability-similarity-direction decomposition with its fixed panel, the architecture-by-initialization design, the CIFAR-100 and ImageNetV2 panels, the gradient-alignment test, the protocol identity with its measured premise, and predictions registered before measurement. It also corrects one claim of the earlier version: the direction of the asymmetry is not stable once the architectures are matched in clean accuracy.

Section~\ref{sec:related_work} reviews related work, Section~\ref{sec:methodology} defines the design and the decomposition, and Sections~\ref{sec:experiments} to~\ref{sec:conclusion} report, discuss and summarize the results.
